# THE MAN WHO FELL OUT OF THE SKY

### Chapter Two: Bread and Questions

---

**I. THE FIRE**

They burned their dead in the evening.

Gojo watched from a distance, hands in his pockets, saying nothing for the first time since he had fallen out of the sky.

There were too many bodies to carry home. So the living laid them in rows on the grass, took their armbands and their blades, and stacked wood over what was left. One hundred and sixty people. Some of them were very young. The soldiers who survived them moved slowly now, the fight gone out of their arms, but nobody sat down and nobody cried where others could see. They worked. That was all.

Then they stood in lines before the fire, and every single one of them put a fist over their heart.

"I offer my hearts to the dead!" Erwin's voice carried across the dark plain, flat and clear. "Their lives, and their deaths, belong to each of us. We will carry them. This I swear. Dedicate—"

"—your hearts!" Two hundred voices, cracked and tired, gave him the words back.

Gojo didn't know the salute. He didn't copy it. He just stood in the grass with the firelight on his blindfold and watched a whole army say goodbye to its people, and thought about how quiet it was. Where he came from, sorcerers died alone in alleys and classrooms, and mostly nobody lined up at all.

*They don't even complain,* he thought. *One hundred and sixty dead in a day and they just... keep the promise. Carry them.*

In his chest, in the big quiet room where he lived alone, something old and tired turned over.

*Suguru would have had something smart to say about this world,* he thought, and then put the thought down, like glass with a crack in it. Suguru would have said this whole world was proof of what he believed. That was the worst part. It was getting harder to argue.

Hange watched the stranger watch the fire. The squad leader stood in the shadow of the supply wagon, arms crossed, cracked glasses throwing back little pieces of flame.

"Moblit," Hange said quietly. "Look. He's not talking."

"He talked for six hours straight, squad leader."

"Exactly. He stopped for *them*." Hange pushed the glasses up. Something moved behind them that was almost gentle. "Write that down. First real fact of the day."

---

**II. LUNCH, SORT OF

Morning came up gray and cold, and the column formed to march home.

Gojo walked among them. That alone broke everyone's brain a little. The man who had erased a thousand Titans walked in the middle of the column like a farmhand on his way to market, blindfold back on, hands loose, chatting.

The food was a tragedy and he said so.

"Okay. Okay okay okay." He turned the hard ration bread over in his fingers, held it up to the gray sky, and studied it like a jeweler with a bad diamond. "This is a brick. You gave me a brick. I asked for lunch and you gave me *construction material.*"

"It keeps for three months," Connie said, wounded.

"So do rocks!"

"It's better if you dip it in the soup," Sasha said. She said it the way a priest talks about holy things. Then she showed him — dip, wait, let it soak — and watched his face with total, serious hope.

Gojo bit down. His eyebrows went up above the blindfold.

"...It's still bread," he said. "But it's *warm* bread. Sasha. Sasha, you're a genius."

"Right?" She beamed, cheeks full. "The soup is the best part. I made it. Well, I found things for it. Found is kind of making."

"Finding *is* making. That's my whole life you're describing."

And just like that, the two most food-obsessed people on the plain found each other, and Gojo Satoru made his first friend in the new world, and it was Sasha Blouse, over soup, in the ruins of the worst day of her life. She would never once regret it. Later, much later, other people would.

Jean watched this happen with his arms crossed and his jaw tight.

"Unbelievable," he said. "He fell out of the sky. He killed a thousand Titans. And he's *flirting with bread.*"

"He's kind of amazing," Connie said.

"He's kind of *insane.* Those aren't the same thing."

"They can be," Armin said quietly, a few steps behind them, watching the blindfolded man make Sasha laugh so hard she choked on soup. Armin had been watching all morning. Armin had a page in his head where the facts went, and the page was getting full, and almost none of the facts fit together yet.

*He eats. He sleeps — did he sleep? He slept sitting up, against a wagon wheel, like it was nothing. He jokes. He stopped joking for the dead. He talks to everyone the same way — soldiers, recruits, no difference. He never asks where the bathroom is. He never once looks lost.*

*Except,* Armin thought, *when he looks east. When he looks at the walls.*

Jean was still going. "—and another thing, why is his cloak *clean?* We waded through Titan blood for an hour. I have blood in places I don't want to discuss, and he looks like he's on his way to a party—"

"It's the one thing I like about him," Levi said.

The whole group went stiff. The captain had come up behind them with zero sound, the way he did, holding a cup of tea and looking at Gojo across the column with narrowed eyes.

"Sir?" Jean said.

"He's clean." Levi sipped his tea. "Battlefield's a pigsty. He walks out of it spotless. First man in this whole rotten outfit with basic hygiene." A pause. the steam off the cup. "I hate everything else about him."

He walked away. The group breathed again.

"I think that's the nicest thing I've ever heard him say about anyone," Connie whispered. "Except Petra."

---

**III. QUESTIONS UNDER WAY**

Hange Zoë had been patient for fourteen whole hours, which was a record, and now the patience was over.

"SO." Hange fell into step beside Gojo with a notebook already open and Moblit trailing behind like a man walking to his own funeral. "Question one. The cloth. The blindfold. You fight blind. You killed Titans while *juggling words* — I counted, you were mid-sentence for at least thirty of the kills. So. How do you see?"

Gojo smiled pleasantly ahead. "Who said I see?"

"You dodged a hand the size of a—"

"Maybe I listen."

"You dodged a Titan *behind* you while looking at *me.*"

"Maybe I'm very popular. Things want to be near me."

Hange made a sound like a kettle and wrote something down with such force the paper tore. "Subject refuses— no. Subject *plays* — Moblit, write 'plays,' that's data too — Question two. The bites. The jaws stopped. I watched jaws stop. There was no armor. There was no sound. Titan jaws do not *stop.*"

"Strong teeth," Gojo said. "Weak manners."

"QUESTION THREE—"

"Hange." Erwin's voice, from the head of the column. Not loud. It landed anyway. "Let him breathe. We're a day out from the wall. There will be time."

Hange subsided, vibrating, writing three questions that did not exist yet. Gojo tipped his head toward the commander, interested.

"You're the boss, huh. The eyebrow guy."

"People say so."

"I like you. You're quiet like a guy who's counting cards." Gojo's grin flashed under the blindfold. "I'd never play poker with you. I want that on the record."

Erwin Smith looked at the stranger for a moment, and something behind his eyes — the thing that was always working, always trading, always asking *what does this cost and what does it buy* — ran its numbers and came back with nothing it could read.

"I'd like to talk tonight," Erwin said. "After camp. Just the two of us. You have questions about this world. I have questions about you. Fair trade."

"Ooh. A trade." Gojo rocked on his heels. "Sure. But I warn you, I'm a terrible negotiator. I always just take what I want."

"Then we have something in common," Erwin said, and walked back up the column, and it was not a joke, and both of them knew it.

---

**IV. THE THING IN THE KID**

Camp that night was a ring of fires on a hill without a name.

Gojo sat where he could see everything, because that was just how he sat. Six Eyes, even under cloth, gave him the camp in layers: two hundred small heartbeats of fear, banked low like coals; the horses; the dark plain, mostly empty now, a few wandering Titan-shapes far off, their little person-candles burning in their necks, miles away, ignored by everyone — which was its own quiet horror, when you thought about it. Nobody looked at them. Two hundred people who fought these monsters every week had already stopped seeing the ones left on the horizon.

*Used to be somebody's kid,* Gojo thought, and let it go, because that road had no end.

Erwin came to his fire with two cups and a loaf and sat down across from him like a man with all night.

They traded. It went like this:

Erwin gave him the world in plain pieces. Humanity behind three walls, a hundred years, no outside. Titans eat people, only people, reason unknown. Kill cut is the nape, one meter long, ten centimeters deep. They come at daylight, they're slow at night, some are fast and wrong and they call those abnormals. The Survey Corps goes outside so that someday nobody has to. The government inside likes the walls exactly the way they are.

Gojo listened, asked sharp small questions — how many people, how much land, who rules, what's the army budget — and Erwin answered some and put some in his pocket for later, and watched the stranger do the exact same thing back, and privately enjoyed it, the way chess players enjoy each other.

Gojo gave back what he felt like giving. A name. A country, very far, "so far it's not funny." Monsters, yes — but different ones, born different. He paused there, picking his words for once.

"Where I'm from," he said, "monsters are made from people. From the bad parts. Fear, hate, grief — enough of it in one place, for long enough, and it... clumps. Becomes a thing with teeth. We fight those."

Erwin went very still. Across the fire, the flames popped.

"You're saying monsters are born from human feeling."

"I'm saying people are messy." Gojo shrugged, light again. "Some of it spills."

He could see Erwin building the thought — Gojo watched it happen behind the man's pale blue eyes, watched him look at the fear-soaked world around them and do the math. *A world drowning in fear. If that man is telling the truth, what does a world like this grow?*

"You said the sky tore," Erwin said instead, out loud, which told Gojo the man had decided not to show him that particular card yet. "You fell through. Do you know how?"

"Nope."

"Can you go back?"

"Nope." A beat. The firelight sat on the blindfold. "I'm not crying about it. Interesting place. Good soup."

"Interesting," Erwin repeated. He turned his cup once in his fingers, a small slow movement, the only tell he had. "Then you'll need a place in this world. And this world will want to know what you are. The capital is already asking. I've held my report for one day. I won't hold it forever."

"Threat?"

"Price."

Gojo laughed, delighted. "See, this is why I like you."

"One more trade, then." Erwin leaned forward, and the fire put shadows in the lines of his face, and for a second he looked less like a commander and more like a man standing at the edge of the question he'd been chasing his whole life. "Yesterday, on the ridge. You looked at my soldiers and you said 'the pen.' Then you stopped yourself." He watched the blindfolded face, waiting. "What did you mean?"

Gojo said nothing.

Wind moved the fire. Somewhere down the hill, a horse complained about the cold.

"Three walls," Gojo said at last, lightly, getting up and stretching like the conversation was a nice meal that had ended. "A hundred years. A whole species in a cage, being farmed for fear — because somebody, somewhere, is getting *something* out of all this scared." He rolled his neck, casual as weather. "I've seen farms before. That's all it was. A thought. Forget it, eyebrow guy."

Erwin sat by his fire alone for a long time after the stranger walked away into the dark, and did not forget a single word.

---

But the moment that mattered, the one the whole camp talked about afterward in low voices, happened before the fire went out.

Eren had been staring all day. Everyone knew. Mikasa had spent the day riding a slow circle around that staring like it was a fire she could put out by standing near it. Now the boy came across the camp with his jaw set the way boys set their jaws before doing something dumb, guards trailing him at Erwin's nod — and stopped in the firelight in front of the strongest man in two worlds.

"I saw what you did," Eren said. No hello. His voice shook and he pushed it forward anyway. "On the plain. All of it."

"Loud, right?" Gojo said.

"You killed them like it was *nothing.* Like they were nothing." Eren's fists were white. There were tears standing in his eyes, angry ones, the kind that don't ask permission. "People die every day out there. Every single day. My mother— they took my mother, and the whole world just says that's how it is, endure it, hide, endure it—" His voice cracked. He hated that it cracked. "And you just— you *laughed.* You laughed and they *died.* Why do you get to—"

He stopped himself. Gojo waited, head tilted, saying nothing, and the silence was somehow kind.

"...Teach me," Eren said, low. There it was.

"You turn into one."

The words dropped into the camp like a stone into a well. Nearby, half-hidden by a wagon, Levi's whole body changed — small, still, ready. Erwin, ten steps away, went frozen mid-step. Mikasa's hand found her blade without a sound.

Gojo hadn't even turned his head toward Eren. He was looking at the fire.

"Don't make that face, I'm not reading your mail," he said. "I just see... shapes. What things are. It's a thing I do. And you, kid, have got something *enormous* asleep in you. Same shape as the giants out there." Now the blindfold turned, and found Eren, and somehow even covered the look underneath it landed like a hand on the boy's shoulder. "But awake. Angry. Locked in the basement and yelling. Am I close?"

Silence. The fire popped. Around the camp, soldiers who had heard nothing — because this was said quietly, only for the few — stood with their hearts stopping one by one. Erwin's one card, the secret the capital had nearly executed a boy over, laid on the table by a stranger who hadn't even been looking at it.

*He didn't guess,* Armin thought, cold to his spine, from the shadows where he stood. *He perceived. He knows things without being told. Without looking. Commander — we can't hide anything from him. Ever. Anything.*

"We need to talk about what you just said," Erwin said quietly, arriving out of the dark with Levi at his shoulder like a knife that had opinions.

"Sure, later." Gojo waved a hand, already bored, already wandering toward the soup pot, because that was who he was. "Kid."

Eren looked up. Jaw still set, eyes still wet.

"It's not a gift, you know," Gojo said, not unkindly, with his back already half turned. "Being the one who can. Everyone you save will look at you like a wall looks at weather. Grateful. And far away." A beat. The blindfold tipped, just a little, like an eye glancing back. "Get strong anyway. It's better than the alternative. I checked."

He went and found Sasha and argued with her about whether soup was a drink, and the camp slowly remembered how to breathe, and Mikasa looked at the back of Eren's head, at the look on his face — the light in it, the terrible hungry light — and moved one more degree between him and the stranger, and said nothing at all.

---

**V. THE ONES WITH SECRETS**

Far down the hill, at the edge of the camp where the picket fires were small, two soldiers stood watch together and spoke without moving their lips much, the way men speak when the night might be listening.

"Did you hear," Bertholdt said. His voice was almost gone. "What he said to the boy. He knew. Nobody told him and he *knew.*"

"I heard." Reiner stood like a stone in a Survey Corps cloak. In the dark, off duty, with only Bertholdt to see, the soldier had drained out of his face and left something young and scared behind. "He reads what things are. Whatever that means. From a distance. Through walls, maybe."

"He looked at me today. At noon. For one second." Bertholdt's hands would not stay still, and he looked at them like they belonged to someone else. "Reiner. If he looks at me and sees *her*—"

"He won't."

"He looked at Yeager and *saw.*"

"He saw something big and angry sleeping in an angry kid. That's a trick, not a mirror." Reiner said it flat, said it like an order, said it like a prayer. Then, quieter: "And if he does see... what are you going to do about it? Look at the plain, Bertholdt. Look at what the plain looks like now."

Bertholdt looked. In the dark, far off, the bowl-shaped scar in the earth was a deeper black against the black of the grassland, and the ground around it was bare where a thousand Titans had stood and run and been pulled into a point no bigger than a marble.

"Nothing," Bertholdt whispered. "There's nothing anyone could do about it."

"That's the mission," Reiner said. It came out of him wrong, cracked at the end, and he stood alone in it for a second before the soldier's voice came back and covered the hole. "We can't take the Founder back with *that* standing next to it. Not by plan. Not by force." A slow breath. "So we do what soldiers do. We smile, we follow, we wait. If this world breaks because of him..." His eyes found the distant dark line of the wall against the stars. "...then we move in the crack. That's all. That's the whole plan now."

Neither of them said the third name. Neither of them had to. Far north, inside the shining interior district of Stohess, in a Military Police uniform, with her boots up and her face as smooth and empty as still water, the girl they were both thinking of was already learning the news.

---

**VI. STILL WATER**

In Stohess, the news arrived the way news always arrived in the Military Police: with wine, and wrong.

"The Survey Corps?" A mustached officer laughed into his cup, feet up on the desk, sunlight through the blinds striping the懒 — striping the lazy gold of the interior. "Gone. Every last one, that's what I heard. Ran into a horde east of Karanese and that was that. One less headache for the budget, eh?"

"That's not what I heard." A younger officer, desk beside, not looking up from cleaning his nails. "My cousin guards the Karanese gate. He says survivors came home. Says a *hundred and sixty* died and the rest lived because—" He paused, savoring it, a man with the best story in the room. "Because something killed every Titan on the plain. Every one. Alone."

"Cannon did it, probably."

"No cannon, he says. A *man,* he says. Fell out of the sky, he says." Laughter around the office. He let it run, then leaned back: "Black blindfold. Killed maybe a thousand of them by himself. The recruits are calling him the Man Who Fell Out of the Sky. I'm telling you, by next week the baker's putting him on bread."

More laughter. The story went around the office and got bigger each lap, the way stories do, and nobody in the golden room of the Military Police believed one word of it.

At the desk by the window, Annie Leonhart did not laugh.

She sat with still, folded hands and a face like a closed door, and looked out at the sunlight on the clean streets of the interior, and did not exist at all for anyone in the room, which was exactly how she had survived this long, and inside the still water of her, everything was moving very fast.

*A thousand Titans. Alone. Blindfolded.*

*Nothing does that. Not a person. Not a shifter. Not even the—*

She made herself stop the thought there, made her hands stay folded, made her face stay shut. But deep under, in the cold place where she kept the plan — the female operation, the timing, the route to Eren, all of it built on a world with rules — one load-bearing rule quietly cracked.

*The plan needs the world to make sense,* she thought. *And it just stopped making sense.*

The office laughed on. The sunlight lay golden on the floor. Annie Leonhart watched the street and went on not existing, and started, that very hour, without admitting it even to herself, to build a second plan with smaller and quieter doors.

---

**VII. THE WALL**

They came up to Karanese near dusk on the second day, and the wall rose out of the plain to meet them.

Gojo saw it from a long way off. Everyone did. But where the soldiers saw home, or safety, or a cage, depending on the soldier, Gojo Satoru slowed down, and then stopped walking, and tipped his blindfolded face all the way up like a man at the foot of a mountain.

Sixty meters of stone, fifty kilometers away an hour ago, now here, filling a third of the sky. A pencil stroke at noon. A world now.

*Huh,* said Six Eyes.

That was all it said. But he stood there in the road and let it pour through him — the stone, the miles of it, north and south until the curve of the earth took it, and the thing about it, the small wrong thing about it that his eyes had been trying to tell him since he fell into this world, now close enough finally to *shout.*

The crowd at the gate had stopped too. The whole column had. Two hundred tired soldiers stood in the evening light and watched the man who'd saved them stand in the road before their wall, motionless, the way you stand when you hear a sound in a house you thought was empty.

"Oi," Levi said, coming back down the line, voice flat, hand nowhere near his blades and also exactly near his blades. "It's a wall. You've seen walls."

"Sure," Gojo said softly.

"Problem?"

"...Nope."

He started walking again. The column moved. The gate of Karanese ground open ahead of them, huge and slow, and beyond it waited the crowds that always waited — the families, the hopeful, the people with scraps of paper describing missing sons by their scars — and the whisper went ahead of the column like a wave, *survivors, they survived, the sky man, it's true, the man who fell out of the sky—*

And Gojo Satoru, nineteen years old, sorcerer, special grade, the strongest creature in a world that had never met him, walked toward the gate of a walled city with his hands in his pockets.

*Even the walls here are liars,* he thought.

He was smiling when he passed under the stone.

The wall said nothing. Walls are very, very good at that.

But far beneath the streets he walked, beneath the cellars and the graves and the old dark of the world, something that had been still for one hundred and seven years felt him cross the threshold — felt him the way a spider feels the farthest thread — and opened one vast, patient eye.

*Two lights now,* it thought, in no language. *Two.*

The gate closed behind him.
