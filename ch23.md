Placed after the finale — the wedding and the night that follows, both of them grown, written in the story's own register: intimate, adult, tender, and tasteful. Chapter 27.

# THE MAN WHO FELL OUT OF THE SKY

### Chapter Twenty-Seven: The Room

---

**I. THE WEDDING**

They held it at the coast, in the first week of the second spring, on the white sand where the gold thread ran brightest in the shallows — because the Queen of the Walls had read exactly one line of the ceremonial charter the court drafted for her, struck the rest, and written her own under it: *the sea stands witness, or no one does.*

The sea stood witness.

It came up the beach in the morning light with the thread of gold running through the long swell like a vein in living stone, and it did not behave itself once, all day — it threw light on the tents, it stole Sasha's ceremonial hat (doctrine; it was reclaimed; relations resumed), it put salt in Mike's autopsy of the wind ("smells like the beginning; still not elaborating"), and once, during the vows, when two thousand people on the sand went silent at last and the seam-voice said *I'm keeping you* and the sunshine-voice said *noted* — the gold in the shallows gathered, bright, brief, like a curtsy or a laugh or a small free ghost standing up in the waves to see, and every soul on that beach who had been at the root of the world felt their spine understand it, and nobody said one word.

The roster of the day ran long, because the family did.

Sasha catered three kingdoms into a single transcendent feast, and wept through her own soup course, and had to be given a chair of honor and a doctrine of honor, and sat in both. Levi gave a toast of eleven words — "He's an idiot. She knows. Somehow it works. Congratulations." — which the veterans present confirmed was the longest speech of his recorded life, and which he delivered staring at the horizon with his boots already touched once by the tide, on precedent. Mikasa's toast was three words and a raised cup — "Contested. With respect." — and the way she said it made it the warmest thing said all day, and she looked at the bridegroom when she drank, and at the bride, and meant it at both, and the iron filing cabinet of her whole heart stood open in the sea light, unbothered.

Pieck's gift was a file, formally closed, stamped with the eastern alliance seal: forty pages, four years, one subject, ending in a single signed line of professional finding — *recommendation: retain permanently; disposition: the weather is a strategic national spouse; morale: unsupportable in the best way* — and under it, unwrapped separately, small and ridiculous and perfect: a tin of the leaf. The correct leaf. The brand he'd mentioned once, on a cold morning, in a treaty house, to an analyst who filed everything.

Zeke's gift was a baseball, oiled and white and inscribed in a coach's careful hand: *for the household — grip it like a family, not a war.* Eren's was nothing at all, delivered at the top of his lungs from the best man's mark, arms spread to the whole unwalled sea: *WE'VE GOT ALL THE TIME ANYBODY EVER MADE* — which was, everyone agreed, the only present that had ever actually been needed. Annie brought her father, who cried into his cap at the queen of the island that had sent his daughter a second bowl, and who informed the bridegroom, gravely, over the feast, "You're too tall. She'll get a neck ache. I'm not advising anything. I'm just saying what a doctor would say" — and Annie, beside him, Instructor Leonhart, hero of the rear guard, made a sound that thirty people would swear to their graves was almost a laugh.

Ymir of the Jaw stood guard at the bride's shoulder through all of it in the dress uniform of the Queen's Own, sixty years and one ocean in her eyes, and when the rings — plain island gold, cast from a certain plate the bridegroom had once taken out of a palace in front of the whole government — were exchanged, she leaned down to her small queen's ear and said the thing she'd crossed a war in a cage to be alive to say:

"Live big, Historia. That's the whole report. *Live big.*"

And Historia Reiss — orphan of a farmhouse and a drawer, spare child of a house of chemists, queen of a country she had never wanted and then rebuilt in her own image, seam all the way forward in the sea light with salt in her hair and the room she had decided to marry standing beside her grinning like sunrise with a ring on — did the single most unqueenly thing ever entered into the minutes of the walls, and was applauded for it by two thousand people and one gold thread in the water:

she laughed.

---

**II. THE NIGHT**

The house they'd built stood alone on a bluff over the sea, south of the new port — small, by the standards of both their lives: white walls, a deep porch, a kitchen Sasha had personally consecrated, and a bedroom whose one window faced west, for the evenings, when the water burned.

They came up to it at dusk, alone at last, through a garden still learning to be one — and the door, when they reached it, turned out to be the day's last joke and its truest ceremony both, because the strongest man in two worlds stopped on the threshold, turned, and held out his hand, palm up, empty, the gesture, the oldest one, the whole story's one — the gate, and the tideline, and the root of the world — and said:

"Carrying you over is *traditional,* apparently. Sasha briefed me. There are doctrines. But you're a Reiss, and Reisses decide their own doors, so—" the grin, softer than she'd ever heard it, "—Your Majesty. Ma'am. Seam. Would you like to open it?"

Historia looked at the door of the small white house over the sea. At the hand. At the last four years of sleeves and treaties and drawers and schedules, all of it arriving at a plain wooden door on a bluff with evening coming on gold behind it.

She took his hand — the actual hand, all five fingers, hers now, on the minutes, in perpetuity — and opened her own door, and walked into her own house, and pulled the weather in after her by the collar.

The room did what rooms do at the ends of long stories: it held them.

For a while they stood at the west window, not doing anything, watching the sea burn down — the day's whole roaring cast gone quiet somewhere below the bluff, the lamp unlit, the gold thread in the water doing its slow breathing — and the quiet between them was the other kind, the kind they had spent four years building out of wells and stairwells and treaty tables: the kind with nobody performing in it.

"Hi," said Gojo, at last, to the window, to the evening, to the fact of her — and it came out with all the sunshine gone, and what was under it was twenty-three years old and had never once, in either world, stood in a room with someone who was staying.

"Hi," said the Queen of the Walls, and turned from the sea, and looked at him — no crown, no court, no witnesses, the seam and nothing but the seam, in a plain house at the end of the world — and held out her hand for the thing she had been waiting, with a constitutional patience that had astonished two governments, to ask for properly.

"The worst page," Historia said. "You know my standing order. New book, worst page first." Her fingers made the smallest, oldest gesture on the table's edge between them, open, palm up, asking. "Take it off, Satoru. I've seen them at coronations and in caverns and once in the root of the world when you were handing a dead girl the sky. I've never once seen them — " and here the seam's voice went an inch wide, honest past all schedule, "—I've never once seen them look at *me,* in a room that was ours, at night, with no one watching and nothing to hold up. That's the worst page. That's the one I want first."

The room was very quiet. The sea breathed against the bluff.

And Satoru Gojo — who had unmasked for gods and armies, for tribunals and cameras, in battle, in mercy, in the root of everything — reached up, and hooked the thumb, and drew the black cloth down slow, and let it hang from his hand, and stood in the lamplight of his own house, at the beginning of his own marriage, with nothing on.

Not the eyes. The eyes were the same impossible blue they had always been, what the sky was a picture of, the room's one window nobody had ever been allowed — but they had never, in four years, been aimed like this: unperforming, unbrightened, with all the weather taken out of them, looking at her the way instruments look at the thing they were built to find.

"...There," he said, and his voice had gone somewhere it had only ever gone at wells. "Worst page."

Historia came close enough to see the lamplight sit in them, close enough to feel the fact of him — the height of him, the warmth, the extraordinary stillness of the strongest thing in two worlds standing still on purpose — and she reached up, and touched the side of his face, and the touch *landed.*

That was the page, the real one, the one no file and no camera had ever recorded: that the man who stopped the world at a hand's width had been letting it arrive for four years — the chips, the high-fives, the shoulder-pats of a whole army — and that tonight, in the lamplight, with her hand on his face, he was not letting anything. There was no door being politely opened by a doorman. There was just a man, standing all the way in the world, being touched by his wife.

"Historia," Gojo said, unsteadily, and it was not a question, and it was every question.

"I know," she said. "I've got you. That's the whole page, and I've got it, and I'm not putting it down." Her thumb moved along his cheekbone, learning it, cataloguing it, the seam doing its gentle terrible inventory at last on the one subject it had never been permitted: "You held the door open for four years. For a whole country. For a dead girl at the root of the world. Nobody ever—" and the queen's arithmetic went to pieces, finally, completely, splendidly, in a small white room over the sea, "—nobody ever holds the door for the *doorman.* So. Room's mine now. I'm keeping it. I told you I was keeping you, and Reisses decide once, and history rearranges, and this—" her hand slid from his cheek to the first done button of his shirt, and stopped there, and her eyes came up to the blue, asking, offering, sure, "—this is me rearranging. Yes?"

The last of the evening came through the west window and lay down across the floor between them like a road.

"Yes," said Satoru Gojo.

What followed was the oldest story, told the way the quiet tell it — unhurried, and graceless in the ways that matter, and funny in the middle, because they were who they were: her hands learned him like a border she had been mapping for four years from the outside; his learned her like a page of the book he'd been reading longest and never once gotten to hold. The shirt, the pins, the small civil engineering of two people and a lamplit room — each of it a door, and each door opened by hand, at its own speed, with no one anywhere being furniture. He kissed the scar on her palm where a coronation thorn had gone in; she laid her ear against his sternum and counted his heartbeat the way she had once counted a returning army, *one, and one, and one,* and felt it stumble when her hair came down, and filed the stumble under evidence. The blindfold fell from his hand to the floor somewhere in there, unsought, unmissed — the first night in either of his lives he had slept, or done anything at all, with both eyes bare and nothing needing them — and when at last they came together it was with the whole story's tenderness and none of its armor, her hands empty at last on him, his mouth at her ear saying her name like a verdict he'd been delighted to lose, and the room — the room he had carried alone since before the walls, since before the sky, the loneliest room in two worlds — 

— the room, for the first time in its very long existence, was not empty.

The lamp went out itself, considerately, some while later. The sea kept its gold thread breathing in the dark below the bluff. And the two of them lay in the wrecked holy quiet of the first night of the rest of it, her head on his shoulder, his hand open against her back — open, palm flat, fingers spread, gripping nothing, holding everything — while the window full of stars came slowly around to the west.

"...Hey," she said, into his shoulder, eventually, in the warm milk voice, one degree, weaponized, wifely. "You're crying."

"I'm *sea-mist.* It's a coastal property—"

"You're crying on your *wedding night,* and it's the best review you've ever gotten, and Sasha is going to want it entered into the minutes—"

"Sasha," Gojo said, with the last dignity available to him, "gets *nothing.* This is a sealed room. National security. marital privilege—"

"Noted," said Historia Reiss, and kissed him, and put her head back down on the strongest shoulder in two worlds, and slept.

He stayed awake a while longer — old habit, oldest habit, the watchman's inheritance — listening to her breathe in his room, in his house, over his sea, in the first world that had ever actually managed to keep him. Then the watch stood down. The watch, after twenty-three years and two skies and one extremely long door, went off duty forever, secure in the knowledge that the yard was guarded, the roster was fed, the country was hers, the room was warm —

— and the strongest man in two worlds closed his bare eyes in the dark next to his wife, and slept like a man with nowhere else to be.

**III. THE MORNING**

The morning found them the way the best mornings do: badly, beautifully, at an ungodly hour, to the sound of the doctrine arriving up the bluff path.

Two bowls, steaming, on the porch rail, with a note in the quartermaster general's unmistakable hand — *BREAKFAST DOCTRINE. THE WHOLE ROSTER EATS. YES, YOU TWO. — S.* — and the sea beyond the rail doing its gold breathing, and the garden's first bees, and, standing at the foot of the porch steps in the dawn light with her hands in her pockets and her face giving up absolutely nothing except the one degree of it that had been public property for five years:

Mikasa Ackerman, first of the real ones, in running gear, at the end of her dawn circuit, who had timed the last kilometer of it to arrive at this porch at this minute on this morning for one purpose, and who served it now, at parade rest, in the inventory voice, while two bedheads and a bowl of porridge listened:

"Update," said the keel.

Gojo, in the doorway, in a blanket, married, radiant, entirely without shame: "...Still first. Gap to second: *matrimonial.* Roster's amended. Continues indefinitely."

"Noted," said Mikasa — and the pink ears were entirely public, and entirely permanent, and entirely at peace, and she turned and ran the bluff path back down into the morning of the free world, contested with respect, in perpetuity, home.

Historia leaned on the rail beside her husband with her bowl, watching her go, wearing his shirt and the crown of nothing at all, and the seam was quiet, and the drawer was closed forever, and the worst page was safe where she'd put it.

"Hey," she said, to the sea, to the gold thread, to the whole ridiculous rest of it. "Best crowd I ever had."

And the sea — the gold in it gathering bright, once, brief, like a small free hand waving back from the beginning of everything — agreed.
