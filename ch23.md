# The Place Between (working title)
### Chapter Fourteen — Family

**i.**

The hunt for the Prime Minister's son lasted four hours, and the city learned things about itself while it ran.

Esdeath hunted the way she did everything: openly, at speed, by method. She started at the rented room — empty, chair still warm, boots' prints in the dust pointing nowhere. She read the room the way she read snow, and the snow said: *he left laughing.* (He had gone on foot, unhurried, and had stolen a coat off a hook on the way out, because performance required costume.) From there, the watch reports came to her in the saddle — violet light over the tanners' walk at the second bell; a whole watch-post of eight men found standing in the wrong street, hats in hands, ears popping; a fish cart teleported forty meters up an alley and left balancing on its nose, fish and all.

He was performing. Good. Performing things stayed where they could be found.

She rode through the swept districts on the way — the boards over the markets, the watch-fires of the arrests — and passed a laundry street where a tenement scaffolding had come down an hour before in the panic of a violet after-shock, timber across the cobbles, and a woman standing in the wreckage of her washing day, unhurt under a shell of ice she could not explain, shaped around her and her basket like a cupped hand. The watchmen swore no soldier had been near. Esdeath rode past the ice shell without slowing, because it was hers, and it had been made in passing, an hour ago, on her way to somewhere else, and she had already finished not telling anyone about it. (Lesson one, done in passing. The woman would never know. It counted.)

Across the city, under the aqueduct, Najenda listened to the watch-bells and the alarms and did the arithmetic aloud, for her team, over the coffin lid.

"Palace alarms at the ninth bell. Then the General's escort mobilized. Then quiet. Then mobilized again — this time *inside* the walls." She tapped the lid. "The palace is fighting itself, gentlemen. We didn't do it. Let's be professional about it: nobody works tonight, the lists get copied, and everyone sleeps, because when a thing this big falls apart, it falls toward us, and I want us rested when it lands."

Ula, in her warm room behind the grain market, was brought a dinner on a covered tray — roast, and bread with butter in it, real butter — by a polite guard who set it down, straightened, and said: "You will be presented tomorrow. A guest has asked for you by name." She asked which guest. The guard, who had been told to answer exactly this and no more, said: "The one you have already met," and left her alone with the butter and the dark. (She lay awake a long time, in the good bed that was also a cage, remembering a spear point settling into the snow, and a blindfolded man walking away, and she could not decide — even now, even here — whether the story of him had saved her life or spent it.)

**ii.**

Syura had chosen the temple.

Of course he had. The temple steps had hosted the gratitude of the north by day; by night they belonged to him — a hundred white steps falling to the square, braziers still burning at the corners, and above them the old god of the Empire carved in stone, fat and blind and smiling. He stood at the top step with the iron box on his palm like a waiter with a dish, and he had let the watch reports find their way to the one person in the Empire he had decided would be a worthy audience.

She arrived at the fourth bell, alone, on foot, cap on, rapier out of its sheath before the temple's shadow finished swallowing her. She took the steps at a walk. (Charging up stairs is for soldiers. He wanted a show; she refused to be one.)

"You took something of mine," Esdeath said, from ten steps below.

"Your *boyfriend's* thing, actually." Syura turned the box lazily on his palm. The knocking inside it had not stopped all night — ring, knock, ring — and it had gotten under his skin like an itch with excellent manners. "Father owns the Empire, you own the north, and nobody owns this. I'm correcting the ledger. Someone has to."

"You will give it back."

"I'll *trade* it." The amber eyes came up, half-lidded, catching the brazier light; the X-shaped scar between them folded as he smiled. "One duel. You, me, the steps. I've beaten everyone worth beating with a sword, and you've beaten everyone worth beating with the weather, and we've never once been in the same room as a fair question. Give me the fight, General, and the box is yours, win or lose. I only want the moment. Moments are the only currency I collect."

He set the box down on the top step, under the fat blind god, and walked down three steps, and drew his stance — and it was, the Six Eyes would have noted, a genuinely beautiful stance, low and coiled, the stance of a boy raised on private tutors and broken chairs. Violet light woke in the stone under his feet: rings and lines, turning, ten meters across, drawn in a breath. (Shambhala's field: anything inside the boundary, he can throw anywhere.)

"Inside my circle or out of it," Syura said pleasantly. "Decide. Deciding is the fun part."

Esdeath did not decide. She refused the question — and answered with the north's instead. She breathed out, long and slow, and the temperature of the whole stairway fell at once, and frost bloomed across the hundred white steps like a lawn of white fire — and the frost *filled* his glowing pattern, every ring, every line, fine white ice settling into the channels of the geoglyph the way frost settles into window glass. The violet light dimmed under the ice, stuttered.

"Snow records everything," Esdeath said, walking up through her own winter, unhurried, rapier low. "It records you."

Syura laughed — genuinely delighted — and moved.

He was fast; he was trained; he fought the way he did everything, for the beauty of the recording of it, and for four exchanges the night rang with steel: her rapier and his footwork, up and down the frosted steps, violet rings blooming and dying at his heels with every turn as he tried to ring her in, and her refusing every ring — outside the boundary before it closed, always a half-breath to the left, reading the pattern's edges in the frost the way other people read print. He teleported the brazier at her, once, flame-first; she froze it into a falling lantern of ice mid-flight, and did not even turn her head. He teleported *himself*, blink-violet, behind her; she was already turning, because the frost told her where his feet had landed, three words of information written in white.

"You're *good*," he said, breathing hard now, wild-eyed with joy, backing up the steps. "You're the second best thing I've ever —"

That was when the ice took him.

Not her ice. It came up gentle as a tide, around his boots, his knees, his sash, white and absolute, and stopped — a full hand's width from his skin everywhere, the width they all knew now, and Esdeath, half-lunge into the exchange, found her own rapier stopping in the air the same hand's width from a black coat sleeve that had not been there a heartbeat ago.

"Both of you," Satoru Gojo said, from the middle of their duel, "are doing this wrong."

**iii.**

He stepped carefully between them, out of the ice, and looked at the boy first — bound to the chest in a straightjacket of frost, furious, thrilled, thirty meters up a temple stairway under a fat blind god with the way home sitting on the top step.

"You," Gojo said, "went through my host's pockets. That's rude where I'm from."

"I improved them."

"You put the two halves of a sentence in one room and they've been talking all night, and you didn't understand a word of the conversation." He crouched to the boy's level, eye to eye, the way he had crouched for the Emperor. "Lucky for you, I'm the only translator in this world. So here's your moment, Syura, since you collect them: the box is a piece of a Door. Not a figure of speech — a *door*, the oldest working one there is, and it opens between worlds. Your pretty pendant is its grandchild. Both of them together, plus a few things you don't have and can't imagine, could open it again." He let that land, and watched the amber eyes go from bored to bright to burning. "When I'm ready, I'm going to open it. One door, one time. And I'm going to need exactly one person in this Empire who can walk the world's roads sideways to be standing at the switch."

Syura stopped breathing for a second.

"You're lying," he said, without any belief in it.

"I don't lie; that's the General's job." (A beat, while Esdeath, behind him, made a small sound of pure contempt.) "Ask your father what his faulty clockwork has been doing for a hundred years. Or don't, and spend your life being the second most interesting man in the Empire. Your call. I only hire volunteers."

The ice unlocked itself, piece by piece, and folded away. Syura stood in the frost, chest heaving, and looked at the box on the top step, and at the man, and at the General, and did the fastest arithmetic of his ruined, beautiful night.

"Volunteers get paid," he said.

"Volunteers get to *be there*," Gojo said. "Best seat in two worlds. Non-negotiable. Same offer as everyone."

"And if I just take the box and run?"

"Then I'll be sad," Gojo said, "and the General will still be here, and she's slower than me but *much* more patient."

Syura looked at Esdeath. Esdeath looked back with an expression promising him a very long and educational future. He picked up the iron box — it knocked against his palm, once, and he could have sworn it knocked *goodbye* — and tossed it, underhand, casual as a man returning borrowed thunder, down the steps to her.

"For the record," he said, "I'm not volunteering. I'm *auditioning*. Write down the difference."

He was gone in a blink of violet that left the frost scorched purple. The temple stood empty but for two people and a knocking box and a fat blind god, and Esdeath caught the box out of the air, and held it, and it settled at once against her gauntlet — back into rhythm, back into time — like a cat returning to its owner.

**iv.**

They ended up on the temple roof, because it was high, and cold, and hers by right of having frozen it, and because he had said, on the way up the steps, "You've earned a true thing," which was, in her ledger, the largest currency there was.

The city lay below in its light and its murk. The box sat on the lead between them, knocking gently, in time with the pulse at her wrist.

"You said a door between worlds," Esdeath said. "Say the rest."

So he paid the first part of the price he owed her, there in the cold, in a flat voice with no jokes in it.

"The Door was built a thousand years ago, in my world, by a man who wanted out. It opened onto this one. Something of it survived here, and became the seed of your Teigu — all of them, the whole family: your ice, the boy's pendant, the armor in the vault. They're its children, raised by this world. The box is a splinter of the parent." He turned his head toward her, blindfolded face unreadable, voice level. "It pulled me through when it cracked. Most of it is scattered behind a seam that's closing. That box is the largest piece on this side, and with a few of its children — the pendant, one or two others — I could open the way home. Someday. If I choose."

Esdeath was quiet for a long moment. The wind moved her hair; below, a watch-bell counted the fifth hour.

"And you are telling me this," she said slowly, "because I am holding the way to your world hostage."

"I'm telling you because you asked properly. Months ago. You've never once stopped asking properly." A pause. "And because of the heartbeat."

"Explain that."

"It's been keeping your pulse since the balcony. I thought it was listening to the strongest heartbeat nearby. It isn't." He reached out and laid two fingers on the iron, gently, and the knocking steadied under them like a creature at a familiar hand. "It's a working of the same craft as your blood, Esdeath. Old cousins. Your Teigu is a child of the Door's family — it's recognizing *kin.*"

Esdeath looked down at the box on the lead between them, at the small patient sound of it, keeping her time — and something went through her that had no name in her language or any other, because she had spent her whole life being the winter, the weather, the verdict, and had never once, in her life, been *recognized* by anything except as something to survive.

"It is not a hostage," she said at last, roughly, and picked it up, and put it against her sternum, under the mark of her Teigu, where it quieted entirely, content. "It's family. Yours and mine, apparently, which makes us —" she stopped, at the edge of a word she did not have, at the bottom of a night that had taken her creed, her certainty, a boy's paper flower, and now this.

"Custodians," Gojo offered.

"Family," Esdeath said, "is what I said," and would not be corrected, and did not need to be, and they sat on the roof of her temple in the cold, the box quiet between them, the way it had never once been quiet in a hundred years.

**v.**

In a warm office with too much food in it, the Prime Minister heard the night's report at dawn from his son, who had come in through a wall (through the wall; through it) smelling of frost and triumph, and had put the whole story on the table between the plates.

His father listened to all of it — the duel, the frost in the pattern, the stranger in the middle of it, *door between worlds*, *I only hire volunteers* — with his hands folded on his belly and his small eyes closed, like a man at a concert.
When it was done, he was silent for a while.

"You dueled the General of the North," he said at last, fondly, "over a clock."

"I *won* her thing back for her, and I got hired for a miracle, Father, and you didn't know about any of it." Syura, dripping melted frost on the carpet, felt seventeen feet tall this morning (huge with pride) and was enjoying it. "Your faulty clockwork opens doors between worlds, and the anomaly needs *me* to open them. The game just changed hands."

"Mm." Honest opened his eyes. They were warm, and patient, and entirely untroubled, and his son, who knew every mood of them, felt the floor move slightly. "It did. And you did well, my boy. You found the handle." (He meant: my son is now the handle to the door, to the anomaly, and to the General — the whole knot, in one boy.) He reached for more bread, and smiled, and the smile was the real one. "Handles are my favorite kind of news. Leave the witness with me today — the northern girl, the one who tells the story. Our hero's guest has been asking after kindness, I hear. It's time he met the well."

And across the city, in a warm room behind the grain market, Ula was woken by the polite guard, and told to dress well, because today she would be presented — and her hands, steady through raids and cells and sweeps, shook now, because she had understood, at last and completely, what she was for.
