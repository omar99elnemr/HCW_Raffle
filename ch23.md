
# THE MAN WHO FELL OUT OF THE SKY

### Chapter Twenty-Three: The Play Staged Instead

---

**I. THE CITY OF THE LEDGER**

Liberio, in the last month of winter, was a gray jewel on a gray sea, and the delegation of the island of Paradis came into it on a white-flagged mail steamer past breakwaters that had been built to keep the world out and were now, for one historic week, decorated.

They came in force and in type: the Queen of the Walls, traveling as a head of state for the second time in her life and the first time in an enemy's capital, with Levi and Hange at her shoulders and the crown's banner furled for the harbor and unfurled for the history books; Commander Erwin Smith in a dress coat that fooled no one who had ever read a file; Captain Arlert, who had spent the crossing being quietly, catastrophically sick over the rail of the ocean he now contained; Jean, Connie, Sasha, and Instructor Leonhart of the Royal Institution, the last with her father's last letter in her breast pocket — *staying, girl, somebody has to eat the garden; bring me a Liberation Day postcard* — and the fourth-year escort under Flocket Forster, whose operating thesis, filed with his superiors in all seriousness, was that the entire island security detail existed to make sure nothing interrupted the weather, and who had packed accordingly.

And at the rail, in a plain coat with her hair everywhere, Pieck came home.

She had not told anyone on the crossing that it was home. She had told them the tides, the currents, the berthing customs, the names of every tower on the breakwater and which war had built which; she had briefed like the professional she was, four years of files with legs; and then the harbor horns had sounded the mail steamer in, low and doubled, the sound that had run through every year of her childhood like a floor under a floor, and the analyst had gone silent at the rail with her knuckles white on it, and the islanders standing near her had all, without discussing it, found reasons to be looking elsewhere while their delegation's enemy-born guide let her face do what faces do when they come home from the far side of a choice.

"I was going to file this," Pieck said at last, quietly, to the gray water, to nobody, to the biscuit in her mitten. "Coming home. I had the framing all prepared — analyst returns to native environment, observe the leverage points." A breath. "It's not a filing. It's just — it's *loud.* My whole childhood is loud in one note, like a struck bell, and I—" she stopped, recalibrated, said the true thing, because the island had infected her with the habit: "I don't know if the note is grief or relief, and I'd give a year's salary for anyone alive qualified to tell me."

From the deck above her, leaning on the rail with a pastry he had smuggled through two customs regimes, the weather looked down at the city that had built the machine that had built the cage that had built the war that had dropped him, presumably, into his second life — and said nothing at all, and watched her homecoming the way he watched everything that mattered: without looking away.

Down on the main deck, Mikasa watched him not look away.

*He does that,* she thought, at the rail, in the inventory voice that had been running richer and richer gearings all year. *Full attention, spent on someone else's pain, at no cost, at scale. Four years of files on the wrong subject, the whole world, every nation's analysts — and the answer is on this deck, free of charge, twice a day.* She looked at the gray city coming up, her mother's hemisphere, and at the small analyst with the white knuckles, and at him above her, holding the sky's oldest posture: a man minding a bell. *He's ours. It's in the minutes.* The thought had become her rosary. It was, she was beginning to admit in the iron filing cabinet, at approximately minute two of any given morning, not the whole of the truth it used to be — because the minutes, lately, had competition; and Mikasa Ackerman, first of the real ones, had started — privately, at dawn, in the language of people who outlast horizons — to plan for it.

"Your Majesty." The announcement came down from the bow, where the queen stood with Kiyomi and the harbormaster's launch. "Berthing in twenty. The Tybur family's carriages are at the customs pier."

"Then let's not keep the theater waiting," said the Queen of the Walls, nineteen years old, seam forward, wearing the winter light of an enemy capital like armor she had personally forged from a farmhouse, a drawer, and a well. "Remember the briefing, all of you. We are polite. We are punctual. We are impossible." A pause; the ghost of the infirmary, warm milk at parade strength: "And nobody eats anything at the reception before Sasha has vetted the trays. That's not diplomacy. That's survival."

**II. THE DAWN WALL**

The jealousy began, as the best of it does, with a wall and a question at dawn.

Pieck had taken him walking on the second morning — she had the city's silver in her blood and four years of debt to her own eyes, and the delegation's masters of protocol had torn their hair and permitted it, because the analyst was the accredited guide and the weather was the accreditation — and she had walked him through Liberio's old upper streets with the machine running at full depth: this square, where the victory parades turned; this wall, whose brick her grandfather had laid in the war before the war before; this café, where war-college students who never once looked at the server had been served by her mother for nine years, a scholar in an apron, unseen in plain sight, the whole quiet tragedy of a people in oneUnhandled lunch rush. Gojo listened the way he listened — which was to say entirely, hands in pockets, the blindfold tipping at the right moments — and Pieck, guiding the most dangerous being on two planets through her childhood, found herself doing the thing she had sworn to her files she would not do: talking to him not as an analyst at all.

"...and that's the roof I fell asleep on when I was nine, the day they took my application photo, and my mother didn't know yet, and I—" The bell tower struck eight; she stopped under it; the whole city rang once through the two of them. "I've never told anyone that. Wonderful. Four years of tradecraft, unraveled by a bell."

"Bells are load-bearing," Gojo said, gravely. "It's known."

And up the street, at the top of the dawn wall they'd been walking beside, in the gray-gold light, a figure in a scout's traveling cloak had been waiting for one hour and eleven minutes — because Mikasa Ackerman had heard last night, from the escort rotation, *which* streets the walking tour took, and had drawn her conclusions and her boots, and made her move in the only language she trusted: boldness, scheduled.

"Satoru." Her voice came down the street level as a fact. She stood on the wall's old rampart stair with the city laid out behind her, her mother's hemisphere, the crescent stone at her wrist, and her chin at the angle that had once ended duels. "Run the drill with me. Here. This wall, this city — my mother's people built half of it, and I'm never here again, and the ranking has an update pending." A breath, and the ears — the ears were doing the thing — went, in the dawn cold, faintly, incandescently pink. "I want the update to *say* it happened here."

Pieck's pen-hand twitched toward a pocket that held no pen, and stopped, and the analyst stood in her own childhood street and experienced, with total comprehension and no file drawer available, the sensation of *someone else's boldness landing on the same square meter of world she had been quietly rationing.* She had four years of frameworks. None of them covered this. "Ah," said Pieck, to nobody, warmly, miserably, professionally. "*That's* what that is."

"Mikasa Ackerman," Gojo said, looking up the stair at the dawn-lit silhouette of the first of the real ones, and there was something in his voice the files had never captured either, something the two women on that street could each feel in their teeth, "requests the wall." He rolled his shoulders; the chains were gone these three years but the gesture remained, the boxer's stretch, the teacher's delight. "The city that built you half a mile of it. Analyst — you heard the lady. Grade us."

They ran the drill at sunrise, on the old rampart of an enemy capital, two of the most dangerous unenhanced humans alive and the strongest thing in existence playing three-move chess at full sprint, and the city woke slowly beneath them, and the three of them owned the morning entire — and if the running ended, as it did, with the weather catching the scarf-girl's final gambit by two fingers and setting her down on the parapet gently as a chess piece, and saying, in front of the analyst, in front of the sun, "Gap to second: *invisible.* Update logged, Scarf. Bring the next one wherever you like" — and if the scarf-girl stood in that sentence for three full seconds like a struck bell of her own, wearing the dawn and the pink ears and the update — then the analyst standing at the stair's foot got to be the first person alive to observe, close-range, the phenomenon her files had danced around for four years, and she hated it, and she hoarded it, and she would tell Sasha about it in detail, twice, over biscuits.

**III. THE PLAY**

The Tybur theater was a jewel box with a nation inside it: tiers of boxes hung with the flags of the world's powers — Hizuru's fan, the Mid-East's crescents, a dozen minor crowns come to see the devil island invited to civilization — and below them, packing the floor in their Sunday best, the Eldians of Liberio in their armbands, an entire people granted one evening out of the zones to be an audience to their own story.

Willy Tybur took the stage like a man stepping into his family's four-hundred-year-old debt.

He was slight, silver-tongued, magnetic in the way of men who have rehearsed sincerity against a mirror until the mirror blinked; and the play was the play — Helos the hero, the wily Tybur ancestor, the triumph of Marley, the old glories in gaslight and music — and it was good, it was genuinely good, and the boxes of the world watched it politely, and the Eldian floor watched it the way people watch the ceiling of the only room they're ever allowed into.

And in the third act, Willy Tybur stopped the play.

He stopped it mid-aria, with his hand up, with the orchestra dying away in confusion, and stood alone in the footlights in the silence of the world's ambassadors, and did the thing his family had been failing to do since a king built walls out of his own people: he told the truth.

"The hero Helos," Willy said, into the dead-quiet jewel box, "was a story. My family wrote it. We brokered the end of the great war, we hid the truth to survive it, we built an empire on a myth, and we let an entire people carry the cost in armbands for a century, because the alternative was admitting the ledger." He turned, slowly, to the boxes of the world. "Ambassadors: the people beneath your boxes have no horns and no tails and no devils. They have a curriculum vitae of indentured miracles. And this week, on this stage, that ledger closes." A breath; the mirror-rehearsed sincerity gone; something rawer under the footlights. "Because the island you came to gawk at has offered this world a single, unconditional, *audited* fact, and my family, tonight, with the last of the credit our myth ever earned, co-signs it: the power of the Titans ends. All of it. The nine, the walls, the terms, the cages, the bands. The Queen of the Walls and her—" a smile, rueful, real, "—her *weather* have the address of the thing under the world, and they mean to renovate, and Marley's war faction means to stop them, and my family's four hundred years of cowardice ends with me picking, publicly, the side of the ending."

In the island's box, Historia stood at the rail in white.

She had planned to stand at that rail like a state document. What she did, because the moment was four hundred years wide and she was who she had decided to be, was stand at that rail like a girl from a farmhouse in the rain, with her hand on the rail and her whole heart's arithmetic in her face, and the floor of the theater — the armbands, the zones, a hundred thousand unseen servers and roof-sleepers and scholars in aprons — rose to its feet for her, and the sound was the sound of the plaza where she'd said *end it,* multiplied by a world.

And beside her box, at the pillar, watching the crowd rise for his queen, the weather stood with his hands in his pockets and the cloth over his eyes, and Historia turned from the roaring floor, and — with the whole theater watching, with the ambassadors' opera glasses converging, with Mikasa at the box's back going very still, and Pieck in the delegation row below with her breath held in both hands — the Queen of the Walls took one step to the pillar, and took Satoru Gojo's arm, publicly, deliberately, at the theater of the world, in the gesture of a century, and stood there holding it while a hundred thousand Eldians roared.

"Protocol," said the Queen of the Walls, to the front row of the world, serenely, over the roar, in the warm milk voice with the seam all the way forward underneath it, "requires heads of state to be escorted."

"Does it," said Gojo.

"Tonight it does."

"...Noted," said Satoru Gojo, twenty-three, terror of two hemispheres, and did not move his arm, and did not look at the boxes, and missed absolutely nothing — including, at the back of their own box, the fixed star going total-stillness on the word *noted*, and below, one small analyst whispering, into her collar, into her files, into the last unwritten page of her career: "*log it. Log all of it. We are none of us getting out of this week intact.*"

**IV. THE TRAP**

Gojo had been counting the heartbeats under the stage since the second act.

There were forty-one of them, in the understructure where the scenery flew and the stagehands worked, and forty of them were exactly where a stagehand's heartbeat belonged — bored, cold, in rhythm with the ropes. The forty-first was under the stage's center trap, three meters beneath Willy Tybur's family, and it was a heartbeat he had first heard four years ago across a firelit camp in the wastes of Maria: huge, slow, and running itself at half-throttle, the way engines idle when they are trying with everything they have not to be noticed.

*Hello, big rig,* Gojo thought, and did not move, and kept his arm where the queen had put it, and widened, behind the cloth, the instrument — down through the stage, through the forty ropes, through the forty bored hearts and the one enormous lying one — and found what he already knew he would find, wired under the trap in workman's loops that no theater in the world needed: chemistry. Fuses. Enough to kill the Tyburs, the queen, the front boxes, and the myth of peace in one great staged atrocity with the devil island's fingerprints bought and paid for on it.

The hawks had not come to watch the play. They had come to end it.

He told exactly three people, in the roar of the standing floor, without moving his lips more than protocol: *Erwin. Levi. Hold the boxes — I'll hold the stage.* And then he told a fourth, because four years of letting people walk had taught him the price of the fifth heartbeat's surprise: he looked straight down through the theater floor, at the great sad engine idling under the trap in the dark with its fuses around it, and he said — not loudly, but the way he said things that crossed any distance at all —

"Reiner. I know. I've known for four years, and I've never once told anyone, and I'm not going to start with a stage. Listen to my voice. You are not doing this. Not because you can't — because you're *late.* The choosing hour already happened, big rig. It happened in a corridor, and you didn't know I heard it, and I've been holding it for you ever since." And then, gently, the kill-shot of four years: "*Because if I don't stop you, nobody stops you — and if somebody has to stop you, it should be somebody who knows which one of you is standing in there.* Get up here. Door's open. Doors, Reiner. It's what they're *for.*"

For four seconds, under the stage of the world, the two halves of Reiner Braun stood in the dark with the fuses in one hand and four years in the other, and chose — and above, in the sudden silence of a play between acts, a trapdoor opened in the center of history, and the Armored Titan came up through it into the footlights.

Not roaring. Not swinging. *Standing* — fifteen meters of scarred plate in a theater of the world, powder-black and ridiculous and magnificent, holding in one enormous hand a spool of wire and fuse torn bodily from the underside of the stage, and in the other, held out flat and open like a verdict, a struggling hawk in a colonel's coat — General Calvi, architect of the atrocity, caught in the dark by the weapon he had come to coerce, his rage already curdling into the special horror of a man discovering that the leash went the other way the whole time.

"MY NAME," said the Armored Titan, in a voice like a landslide finding its old grammar, "IS REINER BRAUN. I WAS SENT HERE AT TWELVE BY THE PEOPLE BEHIND THIS MAN, AND I HAVE DONE THEIR WORK FOR FIFTEEN YEARS, AND TONIGHT I AM *RETIRING.*" The great head turned; the theater's opera glasses shook; the world's ambassadors watched a Titan surrender a general. "THERE IS A BOMB UNDER YOUR SEATS THAT THIS MAN'S FACTION WIRED TO KILL THE QUEEN OF THE WALLS AND MY COMMANDER'S FAMILY AND BLAME THE ISLAND. I HAVE IT. HERE. AND THE ONLY PEOPLE ON THIS STAGE WHO CAN DISARM WHAT I'M HOLDING — " and here, of all things, the mountain's voice cracked, down the middle, where the light got in, "— ARE THE ONES I CAME HERE TO DESTROY. SO. *ASK THEM.* FOR ME. ONE MORE TIME. PLEASE."

The silence of the theater of the world held for one full heartbeat, two —

— and then Satoru Gojo let go of the Queen of the Walls' arm, walked down out of the island's box, through the frozen diplomats, unhurried, into the footlights beside a scarred mountain holding a bomb and a general — and took, gently, from the enormous armored hand, the whole spool of wire and powder and intent.

"Hi, big rig," he said, quietly, in front of everyone, and the theater heard it because the theater had leaned in that far. "Welcome to the yard."

Then he held up his other hand, and showed the world — showed the boxes, the floor, the armbands, the ambassadors, the opera glasses, the whole gaslit century — a small blue bead, tiny, hungry, turning slowly above his palm; and he fed it.

He fed the bomb to the bead. Wire, powder, clocks, chemistry, the whole spool — unspooling into the blue like thread into a needle's eye, matter walking politely into the little hungry light and *not coming out* — and when the last loop of fuse lifted off Reiner's palm and vanished, the bead closed its fist and went dark, and Gojo blew on his empty palm like a man extinguishing a match.

"There," he said, to the theater, pleasantly, over the special silence of a hundred thousand people and one disarmed century. "Now. Where were we. Ah — right." He turned to the boxes, and the floor, and the world, and the sunlight of him came up to full power in the footlights of the Tybur theater, and the armbands of Liberio heard the rest of their lives begin:

"Somebody's ending your curse. You're all invited. *Try* to keep up."

**V. THE COLLECT**

They brought Ymir out during the ovation — because there was no containing the ovation, and Willy Tybur, weeping openly at the edge of his own stage, waved the program away and mouthed *now, now, nothing tops this, do it now* — and the side door of the theater opened, and after three years and four months and one war, the tall girl with the freckles walked out into the footlights of the world, unchained, in borrowed clothes, blinking, escorted by nobody, because the whole program of her custody had ended the moment the general in the mountain's fist had lost his faction.

She stood in the light and looked around at the theater of the world — the boxes, the flags, the roared-down rubble of every nation's certainty — and did not bow to any of it. She looked at one box.

Historia was already over the rail.

It went into no minutes, the way the true things never do: a queen in white abandoning a head of state's box by its rail, dropping to the stage floor in front of the world's ambassadors, walking — then not walking — across the boards to a tall freckled shadow of a girl who had crossed an ocean in a cage to keep a promise about a napkin-folded childhood, and the two of them met in the footlights at the center of everything, and the tall one got there first, arms out, the sixty-year-old and the nineteen-year-old in one body catching the small one like a shoreline, and what they said to each other the theater never learned and never needed to.

The floor of Liberio — the zones, the armbands, the unseen — roared. The boxes sat in the ruins of their programs. And at the back of the island's party, at ease against a pillar with his hands in his pockets, the weather watched the crown collect its first line item and let his face do whatever it wanted under the cloth, and beside him, unhidden, unwritten, three hearts in three different rows — a queen in a shoreline, a fixed star in a box, an analyst with her pen finally, entirely still — each privately, Maturely, and with total commitment to the coming scheduling war, took note of the fact that he was crying behind the blindfold, and had not wiped, and did not care.

Above the theater, in the shadowed top box where a lone bearded figure had watched the whole play with his ruined arm resting on the rail, the Warchief of a dead program stood very still, looking down at his brother in the footlights, at the queen, at the blue bead's afterimage, at the ending of everything he had been built to build.

And across the theater's smoke-hazed air, over twenty rows of the world's diplomats, the blindfold found him — the way it always found what mattered — and the strongest man in two worlds looked up at the Beast in the cheap seats and mouthed, gently, precisely, four words that Zeke would carry home like a stone:

*One year. I know.*

---

**VI. THE BALCONY**

That night, at the delegation's hotel, on the long balcony over Liberio's harbor lights, the scheduling war opened diplomatic relations.

It began with biscuits — Sasha's third delivery of the evening to the delegation's senior staff, trays of the biscuit charter's finest — and the collision of three trajectories at the balustrade: the Queen of the Walls taking the air before the treaty's midnight session; the captain of the guard walking the perimeter with the crescent stone at her wrist; and the delegation's analyst, cross-legged on the balustrade itself like a cat on a warm wall, because chairs, she had announced, were for people with something to prove.

For a while, nobody said anything. The harbor lights ran on. The city's bells rang ten.

"So," said Sasha, generously, setting the tray down in the middle of the three of them like a flag on a summit, "we're all here for the pudding course of history, and I'm not walking back down until the air's clear, so — " she looked around the circle with the serene ruthlessness of a woman who had fed an army through a war and had no fear left in her body, "—*talk.*"

Silence. Bells. Lights.

"He held your arm," Mikasa said, to the harbor, in the inventory voice. "At the theater. In front of the powers."

"Protocol required an escort," Historia said, serenely, to the same harbor.

"It did not."

"The minutes say it did."

"The minutes," said Mikasa Ackerman, "also say *he's ours,*" and the balcony understood, with a strange warm drop of the stomach all around, that the sentence had just been laid on the table like a card.

"*Ours,*" Pieck repeated, from the balustrade, softly, taking a biscuit, turning it over — the analyst with the whole world's files and no precedent for this one. "That's a load-bearing pronoun, Captain. Plural." She looked up, dark eyes gone, and gave the balcony the truth, because the island had taught her the habit and the war had made it cheap to spend: "I came here to audit a miracle. I've audited everything I was ever sent to find, every weapon and every weakness and every why, and the finding of my entire career is that there's a man under the miracle who holds dying strangers' hands and remembers corridors from four years ago — and I find, at forty-one years of age, at my fourth war, that the pronoun has come to feel — *understaffed.*" A breath; the biscuit, bitten; the professional nothing failing entirely at the edges. "There. Filed. Somebody had to say it out loud or the three of us were going to patrol this balcony like rival warships until the curse ended."

The harbor lights ran on. The bells rang the quarter.

"...Four years I've known him," Historia said at last, and it was the tideline voice, the well voice, the one with no crown on it, and the other two went still to hear it, because they each recognized the register and none of them had ever been its second audience before. "I asked him for the impossible at a gate, and he delivered it, and then I asked him for the whole book, and he's still delivering *that*, and I have — " a breath, the drawer open all the way at last, in company, "— I have already asked him the next question. Weeks ago. It's scheduled. When the collecting's done and the curse is dead." The queen looked at the two of them, straight on, the seam at full width, no weapon and no shield: "I'm telling you both because you're the two people alive who understand the arithmetic, and because whatever this becomes, I refuse for it to be a drawer. So. Now you know. Draw your maps."

The silence this time was the good kind — the kind with honor in it.

"Then I withdraw *ours*," Mikasa said, finally, with the precision of a person amending a standing order, "and substitute: *contested.* With respect." The pink ears, at last, entirely public. "He's said *noted* to me too, Your Majesty. Twice. I keep a ledger."

"Noted," said Pieck, mournfully, hilariously, into her biscuit, "is apparently the *currency.* I've been paid in it once and I've spent a month's sleep on the exchange rate—"

"SCHEDULED," said Sasha, to the harbor, to history, to the tray, loudly, in the voice of a woman who had fed two empires and now had to mediate the third war of the heart she'd personally catered into existence, "is not a WORD, it's a DOCTRINE, and I'm putting it on a menu — " and the balcony broke, all four of them, laughing into the winter night over the biscuit tray of the new world, allied and opposite and honest, the scheduling war opened and the terms humane.

Below them, on the hotel's street corner, a tall figure in black stood in the falling snow with his face turned up — listening to the balcony laugh, cataloguing the four heartbeats like a miser, carrying his own drawer and its one freshly scheduled entry up four floors of stone — and Satoru Gojo, twenty-three years old, the strongest man in two worlds, alone in the snow outside the warmest room on the planet, smiled inside the cloth at the sound of it, and did not go up yet.

Some instruments, you let finish ringing.

---

**VII. UNDER THE WORLD, THE SAME NIGHT**

Far beneath the snows of Liberio and the laughter and the lamps — in the place that has no directions, where the threads of a species's fear had run through a loom that had not stopped in two thousand years — the girl who had been weaving since the beginning of everything heard the world above her change its note.

The fear was still there; it would always be there; it was the warp of the whole cloth. But woven through it tonight, thin and bright and *new,* for the first time since the walls went up: hope. Ten thousand threads of it, a hundred thousand, a whole theater's worth and a harbor's worth and a zone's worth, running down through the dark like rain after a long drought — and the weaver, alone in the place with no directions, set down her shuttle, and held one bright thread up to whatever light there was, and remembered, in the way the very old remember — past the sand, past the ribs of the world, past her own name — what it had been like, before, when she had been a girl who was allowed to want things.

*It knocks,* she thought. *For four years it knocks. And now it sends hope ahead of it, like a courier with a lamp.*

The loom stood still in the dark, for the first time in two thousand years.

And somewhere very far above, in a hotel corridor in Liberio, a bearded man with one year left paused with his hand on a doorknob, on his way to the appointment he had crossed a war to keep — the little brother, the keyhole, the pitch he had rehearsed all his life for an audience of one — and shivered, once, in a warm hallway, without knowing why.

The world had two endings left in it, and both of them had appointments.

The loom, for what it was worth — and it was worth everything; it had always been worth everything; that had been the whole tragedy — the loom waited.
