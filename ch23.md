# The Place Between (working title)
### Chapter Fifteen — Paper Flowers

**i.**

The presentation was held in the Prime Minister's office, at teatime, with a chicken on the table that nobody was expected to eat.

The staging was excellent, because the Empire's always was. Honest sat behind his desk in his stained robes, warm as an uncle, the green stone of his crown catching the lamp. The ink-stained clerk stood at the shelf with a file. Two guards held the door. And in the middle of the carpet, in a borrowed blue dress too fine for her and her hands wringing the life out of its seams, stood a girl of seventeen from a northern tribe that no longer existed.

Gojo read the room before the tea finished pouring. The girl's heartbeat, a rabbit's. The guards' boredom, real — they expected nothing. The clerk's file, thick. And the Prime Minister's attention, all of it, warm and total, resting on him like a hand on a scale.

"Sit, sit, both of you!" Honest beamed. "My dear sir — a treasure! While sorting the poor confused souls of the sweeps, we found a *witness.* The famous one! The girl from the stories — the bridge, the spear, the man the snow would not touch." He gestured with a drumstick, fondly, at Ula as if presenting a dessert. "She has been kept safe, warm, and beautifully fed — you will find her in excellent condition. The Empire takes such *care* of its guests."

(He meant: here is the well. Drink, and owe me. Look away, and prove the kindness a lie.)

Ula looked up at the blindfolded man she had followed in rumor for half a year, standing ten feet away in a warm office, and her rabbit's heart did something complicated — and Gojo, who had been hearing about this girl since a border-town transcript, looked back, and crouched, the way he crouched for emperors and millers' sons, to her level.

"Ula," he said. "You told my story."

"Badly," she whispered. "Every telling it grew. I am sorry. It was the only warm thing we had, on the road."

"That's the best use of a story I've ever heard." He tilted his head. "They've been keeping you safe. Do you want to keep being kept?"

And Ula — who had lain awake all night in the good bed that was also a cage, and all the sleepless nights before it — said the thing she had decided, in the dark, she would say if she ever got the chance to say anything:

"You stopped a spear." Her voice steadied on the old ground of the story. "That night, under the bodies. It was not my night to live. Everyone I loved died on schedule, and the spear stopped, and I have been living ever since on time that was never mine — and no one has ever told me what it is *for.*" She lifted her chin, northern to the bone, a tribe of one. "I should have died that night. I did not. So tell me what the extra time is for, man the snow would not touch — and then tell me what he wants me to be, and I will know which of you to believe."

The office went very quiet. The clerk's pen stopped. Honest ate, slowly, watching, the hand on the scale pressing down with pure interest.

"It's not a debt," Gojo said. "Nobody hands you a bill for living. Extra time is just time — it's for whatever you decide, and the deciding is the whole game. That's all anyone ever gets, Ula. The living get to choose." He stood, and turned to the Prime Minister before she could answer, because the second half of her question deserved to be answered in front of the man it was about. "Here's my decision, then. I'm hiring her."

Honest blinked. "Hiring?"

"I'm a tourist. Tourists need guides — someone who knows the stories, the roads, which end of the city eats people. She knows the stories better than anyone alive; she's been carrying them since the bridge." Gojo smiled his blindfolded smile. "Fair wage. Real quarters. Imperial letter of appointment, if you'd be so kind — your clerks write beautifully. It's much harder to arrest a man's staff than a sweep-girl, and we wouldn't want any *confusion* later, would we?"

For one full second, the uncle face and the real face crossed in the air over the desk like weather fronts — and then the Prime Minister laughed, enormous, delighted, and slapped the desk so the china jumped.

"A guide! Wonderful! *Wonderful.*" (He meant: a leash both parties can see is called a collar of office — and the girl lives in the palace either way, and a retainer of the anomaly is watched twice as well as a prisoner, and cheaper.) "We will draw up the letters tonight. Imperial Guide to the Distinguished Guest — ha! The copyists will want a portrait."

"They'll give me a sword again," Gojo said gravely.

"They will *absolutely* give you a sword again."

Ula looked between them — the monster who fed people and the anomaly who hired them — and understood, the way she had understood on the bridge, that she was standing in weather much larger than herself. But the man the snow would not touch had crouched to her level, and had answered her question like a person, and had not promised her anything except the choosing.

"Then I choose it," she said. "Guide." And then, because she was Ula, and would always be the teller of this story: "But I am changing the endings that got it wrong."

"Deal," said Gojo.

**ii.**

The rooftop meeting happened at dawn the next day, on the aqueduct, because Akame had left a chalk mark on a tea stall, and the tea stall belonged to a man who owed Night Raid his life, and the mark meant *north arch, first light.*

She came out of the dark the way she did everything, already there. Black dress, red gauntlets, sword asleep on her back. They stood on the old stone with the city waking below them, mist off the river, and Gojo drank his tea, and Akame said nothing for a while, because she was Akame.

"Najenda sent me," she said at last. "The palace is fighting itself. The sweeps are filling the granary prisons. Three hundred, then six. He is building a city of hostages, and when it is full, he will squeeze — us out, or in." Flat. Simple. A fact, like snow. "Night Raid is going to cut the hand before it closes. Not tonight. Soon."

"And you're telling me first."

"You stood in a doorway once," Akame said, "and made it a road." She looked at him — red eyes, steady, the drawer she kept him in hanging open somewhere behind them. "We wanted you to know before the knives do. That is all. We do not recruit."

"I don't join," Gojo agreed. "But — Akame. When it comes, I'll be weather." He swirled the tea. "You'll know which kind."

She nodded, satisfied, and turned to go, and then stopped, because there was one more file in the drawer, and Akame kept her files complete.

"The boy in the river," she said. "Fourteen. Crossbow. He tells the story wrong in every tavern south of the river — says a sea-god pulled him out. I checked on him last month. He works the wheel-house. He is learning to read." A pause, flat as her blade. "He says the man on the water had kind eyes. He was unconscious. He could not have seen them." Another pause, the longest Akame had ever taken with a sentence. "I think he was right anyway."

She went. Gojo stood on the aqueduct a while, holding a cooling cup, rich in exactly one way.

**iii.**

Lesson two was held the next dawn on the white sand, and Gojo opened it by holding out his hand and saying, "Empty your pocket. The left one. No — the other left."

Esdeath froze for half a second, which for her was a season. Then she took out the paper flower.

It had been crushed and carefully uncrushed. The boy's flower, from the temple steps. She held it on her open palm, and did not look at him, and the box knocked softly against her hip, keeping her time.

"That," Gojo said, "is the lesson."

"Explain."

"Full duel. Everything you have. Ice, steel, the end of moments if you're feeling dramatic — all of it. One condition." He took the flower from her palm, and pinned it, gently, through the black cloth over her left breast, where a medal would go. "This stays whole. Tear it, wet it, freeze it, drop it — you lose. Keep it whole through everything I am — you pass. You may begin."

"You have lost your mind," Esdeath said, with her rapier already out. "That is a *child's toy.* It cannot survive — I cannot fight *fully* and carry paper. Every technique I own has a cost radius. That is the whole science of war — the spending!"

"Yep," said Gojo. "That's the lesson. Mara was one thousand four hundred paper flowers, and you spent the whole sky on it. So today the syllabus is: one." He stepped back onto the sand, hands loose. "Begin, student."

She began.

The duel that followed lasted, by the count of the watch-bells, eleven minutes, and by any other count, a whole war. *Weiss Schnabel* — thrown wide, to the flanks, angled low, every lance a half-second of calculation, because the old throw was a spear-wall and a spear-wall has a storm radius and the flower lived in the radius. The rapier work, closer, harder than the temple had ever seen — she could not lunge through her own wake of cold anymore, so she learned, in strokes, a new fencing, dry and warm and precise, and Gojo's eyebrows went up over the blindfold, because it was *good.* The needle-mist, once — and she killed it in the air herself, half-formed, because the drift would have reached her own chest. Hail, begun and cancelled. Mahapadma, reached for, held, put back.

She fought the entire arsenal of the north while carrying a paper flower on her breast like a court medal, and the arsenal kept flinching, and she kept correcting, stroke by stroke, learning in eleven minutes a discipline that the temple had never taught and the war had never needed: the size of *enough.*

Twice the flower was almost lost. Once to her own cold breath — frost feathering across the black cloth — and she turned her own lungs' weather aside like a swimmer rolling in water. Once to the wind of his passing — she caught the petal's edge against her wrist, mid-lunge, an impossible little motion, a swordsman's economy spent on a scrap of paper — and heard him laugh, once, out loud, unguarded.

At the end she stood on the frozen sand, hair wild, breath ragged, untouched and untouching, and the paper flower sat on her breast, whole, one petal-edge bruised where her wrist had saved it.

Gojo clapped. Slowly, sincerely, the way you applaud a fencer's touch.

"Lesson two," he said. "What was lesson one?"

"You said if I told you, it doesn't count."

"That was last week's rule." He came closer, and lowered his voice, to the size of the space between them. "A scaffolding fell in a laundry street, during the hunt for the boy. A woman stood up under a cupped hand of ice and did not know whom to thank. It was elegant, General. Nobody saw. Nobody will ever see." A beat. "It counted."

Esdeath stood very still on her frozen sand, caught — furious, exposed, betrayed into honesty by the terms of her own code — and then she did the last thing either of them expected.

She laughed.

It came up out of her like ice breaking on a river in the first thaw — full, helpless, two decades too late — and she sat down on the sand, in her pristine uniform, General of the Empire, laughing at last week's secret and this morning's flower and the whole outrageous shape of her own education, while the iron box knocked against her hip like applause keeping time.

"You are a terrible teacher," she said at last, hoarse, radiant, ruined.

"I'm the best teacher in two worlds," Gojo said. "Same thing."

**iv.**

The Empire posted the notice on a Tuesday, and the drums carried it.

By order of the Prime Minister, in light of the cowardly assault upon the Emperor's joy: the persons detained in the sweeps would be *weighed* — publicly, justly, exemplary — in the Grand Square, in ten days' time. The charges would be read. The sentences would be served. The city was invited.

In the coffin shop, Najenda read the posted copy twice, and put it face down on the coffin lid, and looked at her team, one by one, for a long time.

"Six hundred hostages, weighed in public, ten days," she said. "That's not justice. That's bait with a stage — he wants us to come, he's built the nets into the square, and if we don't come, the city watches him hang the poor by the hundred and learns the lesson instead." She lit a cigarette off the lamp, which she only did when the arithmetic had no good answer. "So. We go — but not to his stage. We go to cut the hand before it closes. Bulat. Sheele. The granary prisons' walls. Lubbock, Mine, the square itself — I want every net he's built mapped by tomorrow night. Leone, Akame — the man who is weather. Take him the date, and take him this: we'd rather stand next to him than under him."

And in a warm office, the Prime Minister, who had set the date for his own reasons — six hundred as bait, a square full of witnesses as the net, the anomaly's leash as the prize — buttered more bread, and was content. On the shelf behind him, humming faintly to itself, sat a violet pendant he had borrowed back from his son that morning. "For cleaning," he had told Syura. His instinct whispered that doors are best opened by the man holding every hinge. (He meant: when the door between worlds was finally opened, the pendant that opened it would be in *his* hands, not his son's.)

The city counted ten days.

On the palace's guest floor, in real quarters with a lock that locked from the inside, the Empire's newest employee — Imperial Guide to the Distinguished Guest, letters signed, portrait pending, one great sword drawn by the copyists that made its subject put his face in his hands — slept the first whole night's sleep of her new life, with her boots by the door and her tribe's small brass ring on a cord around her neck, and outside her window the capital hummed its ten-day hum, and below in the streets, in the dark, small gray things with new eyes drifted toward the square where the stages were being built.
