# THE MAN WHO FELL OUT OF THE SKY

### Chapter One

---

**I. THE SEAM**

*(Japan. Three years after the Star Plasma Vessel. One year after Suguru Geto walked out of the world's last door that mattered.)*

The village had been gone for forty years, but the valley still remembered it.

That was the thing about curses. People assumed they were made of hatred, of rage, of the big loud feelings. Gojo Satoru, nineteen years old and bored out of his skull at two in the morning, had learned they were mostly made of *unfinished sentences*. The landslide had taken the village in under a minute — thirty-one houses, a shrine, a schoolhouse with the chalk still on the board — and everything those people had been about to say next had soaked into the mountain and curdled. Now there was a special grade living in the seam where the town used to be, and the seam had been getting wider.

He stood at the edge of the flooded valley with his hands in his pockets, black blindfold dulling a world of stars down to a softer roar of information, and listened to the rain tap the shrine roof behind him. The roof was the only part of the shrine that had survived, which was funny in a way he didn't feel like laughing at, because the roof was floating forty meters from where it belonged, upright and dry, in the middle of the black water.

Space, here, was origami. Folded wrong on purpose.

"Okay," Gojo said, to nobody, to the valley. "Housekeeping."

He'd come alone. He always came alone now. Yaga had stopped assigning him partners sometime in the last year — *you don't take partners anymore, Satoru, so stop pretending the paperwork asks for one* — and Shoko had stopped asking if he was sleeping, which was her way of asking, and the kid — the kid was ten and glared like a stray cat that had already decided the whole human species was on probation, and Gojo had exactly zero ideas what he was doing with him except that someone had to be, and that someone was tall, rich, and free on Wednesdays.

*If I die here,* he thought, stepping off the bank and onto the water, which held, because he told it to, *Megumi's going to inherit a lot of snacks and a mediocre explanation.*

He didn't think he was going to die here. That was the whole problem with being him. Somewhere between the moment on the stone floor of the Star Religious Group's hideout — Riko's blood, Toji's blade, his own pulse going out like a light — and the moment he'd learned to push it back, death had become a door that only opened from the inside, and only if he felt like it. The strongest. People said it like a title. It was more like a room. A very large, very quiet room, and everyone else outside it, knocking politely, muffled.

Suguru used to be in the room. Then Suguru had butchered a hundred and twelve people in a day and walked away smiling, and the room had gotten even quieter, and Gojo had discovered that the strongest isn't the one who wins. It's the one no one can reach in time.

He put the thought down carefully, like something with a cracked edge, and got to work.

The curse came apart in the way special grades always came apart for him now — in stages of disbelief. It unfolded the valley like a letter first, pulling the far bank overhead so that he stood at the bottom of a well of grass and drowned houses. Then it tried the old trick, the one that had earned it its grade: it didn't attack him, it attacked *the space he was in*, folding the well shut like a book, meaning to press him out of the world the way you'd press a flower.

Infinity said no.

That should have been the end of it. The curse's technique hit the infinite series that lived a hand's width from Gojo's skin and divided, and divided, and divided, halving its way toward him forever — a Zeno's arrow made of malice, never arriving. The curse panicked. It had never met something it couldn't fold. It reached past its own technique, down into whatever hollow place its power was pooled in, and it *pushed* — and space itself, already creased wrong from forty years of hiding a drowned town, tore along the seam like wet paper.

Gojo's Six Eyes, which could count the grains of a blizzard, saw it happen in perfect, useless detail.

There was a hole in the world, and the hole was under pressure. Behind it there was no darkness, which was the strange part — darkness would have been fine, darkness was practically a coworker. Behind it was *weight*. An atmosphere of grief the density of deep ocean, billions of fine threads of terror all running the same direction, like hair combed by a current, all of it flowing inward toward a point he couldn't see. A whole world's worth of fear, and it was *moving*, with a destination, the way blood moves.

The curse died mid-scream, unraveling into black motes. The tear it had made did not close. It widened a finger's width, then another, with a sound like the sky's longest held note — and the pull crossed what was left of the gap and took hold of the seam of *him*.

Gojo could have fought it. Probably. Six Eyes ran the numbers even as his heels skidded on water that had begun flowing toward the hole — the probabilities, the angles, the cost.

And underneath the numbers, in the very quiet room, something he hadn't felt since he was sixteen and the world still had people in it worth being surprised by looked at an ocean of grief with a current in it and thought:

*I wonder where it goes.*

"Sasugru," he said — no. "Suguru would've—" No. He grinned instead, weightless, hair whipping up past his blindfold as the world slid sideways. "Kaeri ni meiwaku miruぞ, Yaga-sensei."

The last thing from his world in his ears: rain on a floating roof.

The first thing from the next one: wind in grass, enormous, everywhere — and under the wind, from every direction at once, a sound like a thousand slow drums. Big things, walking.

Then the light took him, and the seam closed behind the strongest man in another world, and in the dark between — in a place that was not a place and had no direction at all — something vast and old felt him pass, the way a spider feels a fly strike the farthest thread of its web.

For one instant, two worlds' worth of terror turned to look.

Then he was falling out of a torn sky, six kilometers from the eastern Wall Rose, above a plain where four hundred giants were already running toward the place where he was about to land.

**II. THE WRONG COMPASS**

*(East of Wall Rose. Twenty-nine days after Trost.)*

Three weeks before, the reports had started coming back wrong.

Scouts are superstitious about wrong reports the way sailors are about birds. Titans are the most reliable thing inside the Walls — reliably stupid, reliably hungry, reliably drawn to the nearest human like iron to a lodestone. The long-range formation was built on that reliability. Every battle doctrine the Survey Corps had ever written assumed it.

Then the vanguard sections east of Karanese began sending back the same impossible message in different handwriting: *Titans leaving their ranges. All of them. Walking with purpose. Ignoring scouts.*

A farmer in the southeast quarter had watched a fifteen-meter step *over* his farmhouse without so much as turning its head, and kept staring after it for so long that his wife thought he'd been struck dumb. Two sections had observed a pack of three-meter class walking in single file. Titans did not walk in single file. Titans did not walk *somewhere*. Titans wandered, and then they saw you, and then they came, and then you died or they did.

Everything on that plain was walking the same direction, like iron filings dragged by some compass no human instrument could find.

Commander Erwin Smith had read the reports four times in silence, then stood at the map table for most of a night. Levi had found him there at dawn, still standing, hands flat on the map, like a man listening to the far end of a string he'd just pulled.

"Something out there is herding them," Erwin said. "Or calling them."

"Or they know something we don't," Levi said, and scrubbed at a spot of dried blood on his cravat with the expression of a man who took titans personally but filth personally-er. "Either way it's the kind of thing that gets us all killed if we sit behind the wall guessing. So we're going out to look at it."

"We're going out to look at it," Erwin agreed.

They took a compact force. After Trost, the Survey Corps didn't have another kind. The 104th's survivors rode out with them — the ones who'd chosen the Wings of Freedom over the Garrison and the Police, which was its own quiet kind of madness — and at the center of the formation, ringed by Levi's special squad and the barrels of a sanctioned escort, rode Eren Yeager.

The capital had screamed about that for a week. The tribunal had put Eren in Levi's custody; the tribunal had not said *inside the walls*. If something east of Karanese could move every Titan in the south like a hand moving chess pieces, Erwin wanted his one piece of leverage on the board where the question was being asked, not blind and caged at headquarters — and Nile had shouted, and the Military Police had threatened, and Erwin had done the thing he did, which was to let them shout until they'd shouted themselves into agreeing.

"If it goes badly," Levi had said, "the brat transforms, and every Titan within ten kilometers eats him while he's glowing."

"Yes," Erwin said.

"That's your plan."

"That's my contingency. The plan is information."

Now the gate at Karanese ground open against the dawn, and the 104th rode out beneath it into the lands beyond the wall — most of them for the first time since Trost — and the plain on the other side was green and endless and absolutely silent.

Jean Kirstein looked at that horizon and felt his stomach try to climb out through his ribs with its hands up. "Great view," he said. "Really glad I get to die somewhere with a view."

"Stop saying stuff like that," Connie hissed.

"I'm going to find a potato plant," Sasha whispered, scanning the grass with total seriousness. "They grow wild out here. Probably."

"It's grass, Sasha. All of it is grass."

"Grass," Sasha agreed solemnly, "grows from soil. Soil grows potatoes. I don't make the rules."

Ahead of them, Squad Leader Mike Zacharias rode with his head bare to the wind, and had not spoken since the wall. Mike rarely spoke. When he did, people two ranks down went quiet to hear it. He'd been frowning at the east for an hour like a man at a dinner who'd caught an odor no one else at the table could.

Further back, Reiner Braun rode with his shoulders easy and his soldier's smile fitted neatly in place, and underneath the smile he was doing arithmetic that kept returning the same unacceptable answer. *Pure Titans don't herd,* he reminded himself, again, uselessly. *They don't migrate. They don't ignore humans. They obey one thing.* His hands were steady on the reins. He made sure of it. Beside him Bertholdt's eyes were doing their endless nervous scan of the horizon, and Reiner knew exactly which terrifying shape his friend was hoping not to see and exactly which worse one he was actually afraid of.

*If it's them— if someone from home already—*

Not out loud. Never out loud. He fixed the smile and rode east.

Mikasa rode where she could see Eren, which was a fact about her the way gravity was a fact about the ground. Armin rode with his map folded to a square he could hold one-handed, sketching the reports' arrows in his mind again and again, trying to pre-build the shape of the impossible so it couldn't ambush him when it arrived. He'd learned that at Trost, paying for the lesson in other people's blood: his mind worked best fed early.

And Eren rode at the center of his armed ring, caged and burning, staring east with an expression his friends had learned to be careful around.

"Something out there," he said quietly, to no one. Mikasa heard him anyway. "Something's out there making them run. I want to see it."

He got his wish at 11:40 in the morning, and it cost the Survey Corps one hundred and sixty lives before the sky finally opened.

**III. THE RIVER**

The first hour beyond the wall, they saw no Titans at all, and the silence was worse than any black flare.

Contact came within the first hour. Always. Everyone knew it the way they knew their own names. So when the first hour passed clean, and the second, Mike standing in his stirrups with his jaw working at the wind, the veterans stopped looking relieved and started looking like men in a rowboat who'd just noticed the water wasn't making waves anymore.

At 11:40, the vanguard's flare went up: black. Abnormal.

Then a second black. Then, from a section almost directly east, a third — and a fourth, staggered along a horizon-wide arc, and every relay signal the same, until Oluo, squinting east from Levi's flank, said in a voice pitched carefully flat:

"...That's not a signal. Look at the dust."

The dust stood off the horizon like a dirty curtain, kilometers of it, catching the noon light brown-gold. Under the curtain, a darkness was moving. It took the eye a long, unwilling moment to resolve the darkness into shoulders, and the shoulders into thousands.

Erwin raised his fist. The column halted with a rustle of leather and horse-flesh and one recruit's poorly smothered sound of pure fear.

The long-range formation fanned wide and slow, an observer's arc, and for a quarter of an hour the Survey Corps of humanity watched a sight no living person had seen and no book recorded: a *migration*. A sea of Titans — three-meter class packed shoulder to shoulder with tens, fourteens, a fifteen-meter wading through the middle of the flow like a boulder in a river — all of it moving with the purpose of water, east, east, east.

They passed within fifty meters of the nearest scout section and did not turn.

Soldiers sat their horses with their blades half-drawn and their minds quietly coming apart. A Titan's whole existence was *humans*. A Titan would cross a field of fire, tear itself open on a wall, walk into the sea — for a human. Everyone had lost someone to that single-mindedness. To watch one amble past a shivering boy on a horse, close enough to count the teeth in its lazy smile, without the smile even arriving—

"Why aren't they—" Connie whispered.

"It's ignoring us," Hange breathed. The squad leader's glasses flashed; behind them, Hange's eyes were enormous, ecstatic, appalled. "They're *ignoring* us. Moblit, are you writing this down, Moblit, this is the most important thing that has ever—"

"Hange." Mike's voice, low. The wind had changed. "The wind's wrong."

At the eastern edge of the sea of Titans, without any signal any human could name, several hundred heads turned at once — toward the formation, toward the horses, toward the sudden realization rippling through the horde like news through a crowd, the oldest news there was:

*Humans.*

"Oh," Armin said, very quietly, into the silence before the noise. "We're in the way."

The river changed course, and fell on them.

The first minute killed forty people. The reports afterward struggled to hold together, because battle beyond the wall was always a broken telling, but the pieces were these: the formation's right wing folding like paper; the sound the 14-meter made when it caught a scout section leader out of her swing and bit down mid-laugh; red flares going up along the whole line like a festival of the end of the world; Erwin's voice on the relay, flat as a ledger — *anchor on the ridge, fighting retreat, hold the crest, we are not dying in a rout* — the one prayer he allowed himself, because a rout on open ground was simply a schedule of deaths.

The ridge became everything. Open crest, good anchor points, gas discipline. The blades came out.

What Eren remembered afterward was the noise — steel and steam and screaming arranged into a rhythm, like some huge machine designed by a sadist, *chunk-hiss-scream, chunk-hiss-scream*. What Jean remembered was the recruit from his own trainee squad whose cable he saw snap taut through a Titan's reaching hand, the whiplash arc of the body, the moment of stillness at the top — and the hand closing. He didn't remember deciding anything. He remembered flying at a Titan's wrist because it was closer than his grief, and the long bright anguish of the cut, and screaming something with no words in it.

What Mikasa remembered was geometry: Eren's guard ring, the number of Titans angling toward the ridge's center, the half-second margins. What Armin remembered was the pattern — and the pattern was the horror, because it held: *they don't want us, none of them want us, they're all still leaning east, we're not prey today, we're an obstacle—*

"Commander!" Armin's voice cracked across the crest, high and fast, and Erwin's eye found him — a small blond figure on a horse gone mad-still among the flares. "They're not attacking us — they're *going somewhere* — we're in the current, Commander, we're in the river — let us be in the river somewhere *else!*"

"I'm aware, Armin." Erwin's voice stayed level while the world steamed and bled around it. It was the most reassuring and most frightening thing on the ridge. "The retreat line's cut. South flow's across our exit. We hold the crest until the current shifts—"

"—the current's not going to *shift*—" 

"—or until dark. Whichever comes first." A Titan hand closed over a soldier twenty meters away; the steam of it rolled across the command group like breath from an oven. Erwin didn't blink. "We hold the crest."

They held the crest for fifty minutes, and the Survey Corps died at a rate of roughly two lives per minute, and every soldier on that hill knew the mathematics, and every soldier on that hill had known the mathematics on the day they'd chosen the wings, and they held it anyway, blade by blade, because the alternative was a rout, and behind them, behind the wall, was everyone.

Sasha ran out of blades at minute forty and stood back-to-back with Connie, both of them holding spare steel for each other with shaking hands, screaming each other's names with every reload. Reiner hauled a wounded recruit one-armed out of a collapsing pocket with his smile finally, mercifully gone, just the machine of him working; Bertholdt cleared a lane above him with a perfection that had stopped feeling like a person's and started feeling like a clock's. Petra's gas ran to fumes; Eld dragged her line to the center by main force; Oluo bit through his own tongue in the middle of a flourish and kept cutting, blood in his teeth, swearing in a spray.

And at the center of the ring of steel around Eren Yeager, Eren screamed at Levi across ten meters of hell:

"LET ME OUT! I CAN HOLD THE EAST FACE — LEVI, *LET ME TRANSFORM!*"

"And then what?" Levi didn't look at him. His blades moved with the hideous economy of a man who had done nothing else his entire life. He was spattered to the hairline in Titan blood, already evaporating off him in threads, and his voice was the voice of a man discussing laundry. "You glow, brat. Every Titan in that ocean says dinner. We spend you for ten of them." *Thunk.* Steam. "You're worth more than ten." *Thunk.* "Stop volunteering to be cheap."

"PEOPLE ARE DYING—"

"People are *always* dying." It wasn't cruelty. It was a fact he was handing Eren, like a knife by the handle. "That's the job. Breathe."

It was at that precise moment, at minute fifty-one — with the gas reserves of two full sections dead empty, with the eastern slope of the ridge climbing bodies that steamed as they climbed, with Sasha and Connie backed against a rock with one blade left between them, with a fourteen-meter class looming over them, its palm rising with the terrible unhurried certainty of a lid closing over a jar — that the sky rang.

**IV. THE ARRIVAL**

It was not thunder. Thunder is a sound. This was a *note* — a struck bell the size of the sky, laid over the plain like a second horizon — and it carried a pressure with it, a wrongness in the ears, a feeling every living thing on that ridge experienced in its spine before its mind had anything to say at all.

Horses screamed. Every soldier who still had gas grabbed a line and *held*. 

And every Titan within sight — thousands, a sea of them, the horde and its river alike — stopped moving.

All of them. At once.

Steam curled off their shoulders in the sudden quiet. Ten thousand faces, each a different rictus of mindless appetite, turned east with the smooth synchronized menace of a field of sunflowers finding the sun — and the entire sea of monsters *ran*, away from the ridge, away from the humans they'd been devouring, toward a single point on the eastern plain where the air above the grass was —

Armin would spend the rest of his life trying to describe it and land, decades on, on: *a scratch. Like something had scratched the daylight, and the scratch was staying open.*

"They stopped," Levi said. The words fell out of him flat with a disbelief he'd have denied under torture. "They *stopped.*"

Then the sky tore, and light like a white seam split the air over the eastern plain, and something fell out of it.

Not a Titan. Titans fell like sacks. This fell like a dropped feather trying to remember it shouldn't — hair-first, a banner of white hair, a long black shape trailing a blacker ribbon of cloth, tumbling loose and unhurried out of the wound in the sky, and the seam zipped shut behind it with a *snap* that everyone's teeth felt, and the feather —

— landed.

Standing. On the forearm of the fourteen-meter Titan whose palm had been an inch from crushing Sasha and Connie into the rock.

It was a man. He was standing on the Titan's arm with his hands in his pockets.

The Titan's palm hung in the air above two recruits, and it was *trembling*. Its whole arm shook with the full dead weight of its owner's strength, pressing down on — on nothing, on the space above Sasha's head, which refused. Sinew cracked. The arm quaked like a bridge in a storm. The man standing on it shifted his weight, tapped the heel of one black shoe against the Titan's skin — *tak, tak* — the universal rhythm of a customer at an empty counter.

He tilted his head. He was young. He wore a strip of black cloth bound over his eyes like a blindfold, and black clothes that fit like they'd been poured on, and he was looking — *looking*, somehow, with his eyes covered — first at the palm that couldn't descend, and then down, with mild curiosity, at Sasha and Connie pressed against the rock beneath it.

"Hi," the man said. His voice was light, friendly, and carried across the whole silent ridge as if the battlefield were a room and he'd entered it. "Hold on a second."

He raised one hand — a slim, unhurried gesture, like a man hailing a waiter — and touched two fingers to the Titan's wrist.

The arm came off.

It didn't get cut. Nothing struck it. The limb simply *opened* along a line, wrist to shoulder, like a jacket unzipping, and the fourteen-meter toppled forward past the ridge with a sound like a felled cathedral, and by the time its face hit the grass the whole body was collapsing into steam — every soldier on the crest watching the nape of its neck, as trained, and seeing it already destroyed, already sheared open along that impossible line, a wound no blade on this earth had made.

Sasha slid down the rock onto her rear in the grass. Connie made a sound like a kettle.

The man dropped the last meter out of the dying steam, landed in the grass without bending his knees, and stood up in the middle of the end of the world: ridge, corpses, smoke, ten thousand Titans thundering toward him from the east, and a tattered remnant of humanity staring down at him from the crest above.

He turned in a slow circle. Hands in pockets. Blindfolded face tipping up to the sky, then along the horizon, then — briefly, curiously, at the distant line of the Wall itself, fifty kilometers off, a pencil stroke of stone against heaven.

"Huh," he said.

*Satoru Gojo,* nineteen years old, special grade sorcerer, walked out of a tear in the universe into a world he had been falling through for either three seconds or three eternities, and took inventory at a speed that would have terrified anyone able to watch it happen.

*Big sky. Bigger than home. Gravity, check, lighter maybe. Language is parsing — whatever that place between places was, it rearranged something on the way through, small mercies, note for later.* The wind through the grass and the sound beneath it, the thousand slow drums. *Giants. Lots. Not curses.* That registered with a strange relief and then a stranger interest. *No cursed energy in them at all — but there's a little... fuse, in each one. A point. Nape height. Something that used to be a person, wearing a person-shaped monster like a suit.*

Used to be.

The thought arrived fully, without permission: *every one of these giants used to be somebody's kid,* and with it a cold professional weight he hadn't asked to carry on arrival. He'd seen curses made of single murders. This was a *species* of it. A harvest.

And the air. He couldn't stop cataloguing the air. The whole world was soaked in it — fear, grief, despair, a saturation so total it wasn't even an emotion anymore, it was *weather*, it was the pressure at the bottom of an ocean. In his world, air like this birthed curses the way stagnant water birthed mosquitoes. A country this frightened, penned this long, should be crawling with them, special grades growing in the dark like mold on bread.

There were none. Not one. An ocean of suffering with nothing swimming in it.

*So what's doing the eating down there?*

The giants reached him.

He felt — genuinely, pleasantly — grateful. Answers were jogging toward him at fifteen kilometers an hour with their mouths open.

"Rude, though," Gojo said, to the first one, sidestepping a hand the size of a carriage with a movement that didn't look fast so much as *early*, as if he'd simply been standing somewhere else the whole time and the Titan had grabbed the afterimage. "You didn't even introduce yourselves."

Above, on the crest of the ridge, humanity hung from its anchor lines and its last reserves of gas and watched.

A three-meter snatched at the black-clad figure — fist closing — and the fist closed *around nothing*, fingers meeting, and he was standing on its thumb, adjusting his sleeve. A ten-meter leaned down and *bit* him, jaws shearing shut with a crack that carried up the slope, teeth meeting teeth — and he was standing in the grass in front of its face, unbothered, brushing a fleck of spittle off his collar while it chewed empty air and looked, idiotically, surprised.

"Morning breath," Gojo told it. "Do you guys floss? Be honest."

It lunged, mouth wide enough to swallow him and two horses besides, and the assembled survivors of the Survey Corps got the image that would sear itself into a hundred dreams: the man standing motionless in the closing dark of the mouth, hands still pocketed, head tilted — and the jaws *stopping*, inches off his skin, straining, shuddering, the full mountain of muscle and furnace heat behind that bite meeting nothing at all, stalled on the empty air above a man's blindfolded face as if the world itself had said *no* and meant it.

Gojo reached up, almost gently, and patted the Titan on its lowered brow.

"Down you go."

Its nape opened like a zipper. It fell past him, and was steam before it finished falling.

"Did—" Jean's voice came out of the silence on the ridge as a thread. "Did it miss? The ten-meter. Did it—"

"It didn't miss," Eld said. His knuckles were white on his blades. "I watched. It hit."

"It hit," Petra whispered, "and nothing happened."

On the eastern slope, the man in black began to walk — strolling, spine easy, unhurried — *into* the sea of Titans, and the Titans died around him like wheat before something no one could see. He never drew a weapon. He never appeared to hurry. A fifteen-meter's hand descended and he simply wasn't in it and its nape was open; two more closed on him from either side and met each other with a splintering crack while he ducked under their joined arms, still walking, and their heads slid off into the steam. Every kill was the same: the little gesture, the light touch, the opening line, the fall. The field of monsters was thinning around one man's afternoon stroll.

Mike Zacharias stood next to Erwin on the crest, and his face had gone gray in a way the veterans had seen on recruits and never, ever on him.

"Levi," Mike said. The wind had swung around as the horde's river passed, carrying the east across the ridge. Mike had been tasting it for a full minute, the strongest nose humanity had. "That man has no scent."

Levi's eyes didn't leave the field. "Everyone has a scent."

"Titan blood. Steam. Grass. Blood. Nothing off him. *Nothing.*" Mike's voice dropped. "He smells like the moment before lightning."

Levi said nothing. Levi was doing the only thing he trusted himself to do, which was measure. His body — the body Kenny Ackerman had forged, the body that had moved faster than death for as long as anyone had kept score — had been reporting quietly, insistently, since the moment the man dropped out of the sky. He'd watched every exchange. And his eyes, the best in humanity, had managed to track perhaps one movement in five, and those only because the man had *let* the kills be legible, the way you slow down for a child.

*He's not fighting,* Levi thought, and the thought had a bottom to it that made his grip tighten on the wire. *He's not even being careful. He's humoring them.*

Beside Levi, Mikasa Ackerman had gone rigid — not with fear. With *recognition*. Her body had done this only once before in her life: standing in the assessment yard across from Captain Levi, feeling the animal fact of an overwhelming difference arrive through her skin before her mind consented to it. The thing in her blood, whatever the Ackermans carried, was doing it again now, and louder, and it was saying: *that is not a scale of strength. That is a different substance.*

Eren—

Eren was weeping, and didn't know it, and didn't care.

He hung in his harness at the center of his guard ring with his fists clenched white and his eyes blown wide, watching a lone figure in black walk into an army of humanity's extinction with its hands in its pockets, and something in his chest was coming apart with an ache that was half anguish and half *hunger*. Every dream he'd ever had of the sea, every rage he'd carried since the rubble of Shiganshina, every promise — *I will kill them all* — was looking at the plain and seeing, for the first time in his life, the image of what it would actually look like for humanity's side of the ledger to have a *surplus*.

"That's what it looks like," he whispered. Mikasa heard him. "Mikasa — *that's* what it looks like. Being free."

"Stay behind me," Mikasa said, which was not an answer, and repositioned her horse two degrees regardless.

At the command point, Hange Zoë made a sound that Moblit would privately describe for years as *the sound a person makes when they meet their god and their god is winning*. The squad leader's glasses were cracked down the left lens, their cloak was singed, their section had lost four people that hour, and they were leaning so far out over the crest that Moblit had grabbed the back of their harness on pure reflex.

"Moblit," Hange said, in the strained, shaking voice of someone having a religious experience at gunpoint. "Moblit, look at the bites. Look at the *bites*, Moblit. The jaws stopped. Jaws don't stop. Nothing stops — is it his skin, is it armor, it's not armor, there's no sound, there's no impact, there's no *contact*—" Their scarred knuckles whitened on the pen they were, insanely, still holding. "I have two Titans in the yard at headquarters and I swear on both of them I will trade the entire yard—"

"Hange," Moblit said, deathly quiet. "Look at the convergence."

Because that was the other thing, the thing underneath, the thing that would keep Erwin Smith awake for the rest of his life whenever he allowed himself to sleep: the horde was not flowing east anymore.

It was flowing *to him*.

Every Titan on the plain had re-aimed. The sea that had been running toward the tear in the sky was now running toward the man who'd fallen out of it, thousands of them, abandoning the ridge, abandoning the humans, converging with mindless devotion on a single point of black cloth and white hair — and he stood in the center of their devotion with his hands in his pockets looking, if anything, faintly put out.

"Excuse me — hi — yes, you, big guy —" Gojo leaned out of the way of a sweep that flattened a hectare of grass. "Quick question for whoever's in charge around here." His hand flicked; the nine-meter behind him lost its nape mid-lunge. "Anyone? Lady with the glasses on the hill, you look like you run things — no? The enormous blond gentleman with the eyebrows—"

He was joking. He was *clearly* joking, strolling, chatting, killing a monster every ten seconds with a movement of his fingers too small and too fast for human eyes — and two hundred meters away, hidden in the churn of the formation's east face, Reiner Braun stood in the grass where he'd landed after cutting a recruit free, and felt the architecture of the last five years coming apart bolt by bolt.

*They obey one thing.* The thought tolled through him like a bell through a cracked one. *Pure Titans obey the Coordinate. Only the Coordinate. Something on this plain called every Titan in the south — and now it's wearing a blindfold and killing them like it's bored—*

"Reiner." Bertholdt's voice, barely air. Bertholdt had landed beside him and could not seem to make his knees agree to hold. Sweat and steam ran off the tall boy in sheets. "Reiner, what do we— that's not — nothing does that, nothing *human*—"

"Later." Reiner's jaw barely moved. The soldier's mask sat on his face; only the eyes behind it were open pits. "Smile. You're a soldier who's glad to be alive." A Titan passed them at a dead run, ignoring them, intent on the distant figure — *ignoring them, Titans don't ignore, that was the one rule of this world* — and Reiner stared after it and thought, with a cold that started in his marrow: *our mission was measured in Titans. Whatever that is — Titans just stopped mattering.*

On the crest, Armin Arlert stood with his notebook forgotten in his hand and built the only way he knew how to fight something: he built a *model*, out loud, in a whisper meant for himself.

"He fell out of the sky. The Titans stopped fighting us the second he arrived — all of them, at once, and ran to *him*." His eyes flickered, listing. "He wears a blindfold and fights blind. He never looks at the Titans he kills — I haven't seen him look at *one* of them. The bites don't reach him — there's no armor, there's no sound, the jaws just — stop." A breath. "He's not one of them. He's not like Eren. Whatever Eren is — that's not this."

"Then what is he?" Jean demanded. Jean's voice was doing its brave thing, which was sarcasm held upside down. "Because I've been doing the math and I'd like to formally announce that I have *no idea* what I'm looking at, and I'd kind of like that to be on record before whatever it is notices us."

"It's a weapon," Ymir said lazily, from where she was stood in front of Krista, exactly as if she hadn't repositioned herself there six minutes ago and had no opinion on the matter. "Look at it. Governments kill for things like that. Whoever's government gets it first." A thin smile. "Good news for us little people — we're finally somebody's priority."

"Don't say that," Krista whispered, watching the plain with wide, soft, appalled eyes. "He saved them. Sasha and Connie. He *saved* them."

"He did," Ymir agreed, and her eyes never left the black figure, flat and bright as a snake's. "Question's what he saves next."

Then the horde's full weight arrived, and the day stopped being a story anyone had words for.

**V. THE BEND IN THE WORLD**

Two thousand Titans, perhaps more — the survivors' estimates would never agree — packed themselves onto that corner of the eastern plain, a living avalanche funneling down to one point, hands and mouths and shoulders, closing from every side on one man.

The man looked around himself at the shrinking circle of daylight.

Gojo had, by this point, been in the world for eleven minutes, and had killed one hundred and forty of its monsters, and confirmed most of what he needed to know: *no technique, no signs of coordination — just appetite in bulk; nape-candle confirmed, one each; they regenerate, they burn hot, they're strong the way a flood is strong, and there is nothing in this world's arsenal that has any business touching me.* The locals fought them with swords on wires, which was either the bravest thing he'd ever seen or the saddest, and was definitely both. He liked them. You had to like people who chose the wire.

But there were a *lot* of the big ones, and they kept coming, and they were starting to crowd out the daylight, and — this was the real issue — the survivors on the hill had exhausted faces and empty tanks, and some of the giants were beginning, in the manner of fires, to spit off embers: small clots of the horde breaking loose and drifting ridge-ward, toward the smell of all that fear.

Gojo sighed.

"Okay," he said. "You're loud, you're rude, you smell like a sauna in a slaughterhouse, and you're scaring the wire people." He drew one hand, finally, lazily, out of his pocket. "Indoors voices."

Mikasa Ackerman was the closest soldier with a clear line of sight, close enough to hear him under the thunder — and so she was the one who heard the two words he said next, in a language she didn't know, quiet as a man remembering something:

"...Lapse. Blue."

Between his finger and thumb, a bead of light opened.

It was small — the size of a marble, blue-black, deep as a well — and the world *leaned toward it*. There was no better verb and no other true one. Grass tore free of the soil in a widening radius and slid. Steam changed its mind about rising. Sound bent. Every soldier on that ridge felt it in the inner ear, a great inhale on the far side of the sky, and Mikasa, watching from three hundred meters, felt the pull in her blades, in her hair, in her teeth — the whole plain's worth of falling things voting to be elsewhere.

The man flicked it, the way you'd toss a pebble into a pond.

Where it passed, the front rank of the horde — forty Titans deep — lost its footing. Not one at a time. *Together*, a thousand tons of monster slewing off its axes like filings finding a magnet, mouths open, hands out, sliding and toppling inward, and the bead of blue light drifted down into the pile with the patience of a drop of ink in water, if ink hungered — and the pile *closed over itself*. Compacted. The sound was nothing anyone had heard: the groan of a mountain deciding to be a hill, a wet structural shriek of steam and stone-ground pressure, the air itself cracking inward like a vacuum — and then silence, total, ringing, wrong.

Where the densest column of the horde had been, there was a bowl.

A smooth, packed-earth bowl the size of a village square, glazed dark with pressure, steaming gently. In its center lay a knot of Titan-flesh the size of a house, crushed into a density no flesh was ever meant to hold, dissolving into vapor even as they watched, as if the world were tidying it away. The bead of blue light was gone. So was every Titan that had stood within a hundred meters of it.

The scale of the silence afterward was its own country.

On the ridge, a recruit was sick, noisily, and no one looked at him, because most of the others were white to the lips and several had simply sat down in the grass where they stood. Jean stared at the bowl and said, with enormous care, like a man defusing himself: "I have decided not to have any opinions until I'm behind the wall. If anyone needs me, I'll be behind the wall. screaming."

"Gravity doesn't—" Armin's whisper had gone hoarse. "That's not — gravity doesn't *do* that. He aimed it. He aimed *pulling*."

Hange was no longer making words. Hange was gripping Moblit's shoulder with one hand and their cracked glasses with the other, laughing — a small, cracked, mortal sound, one observer's whole heart breaking open with joy at the edge of an abyss — and the pen finally dropped out of their hand into the grass, because both hands were needed for holding on.

Levi's blades had gone still.

He stood alone at the crest's edge, wind in his dark hair, Titan blood steaming off his cloak, and his eyes — narrow, gray, the most disciplined instrument humanity owned — moved from the glazed bowl, to the compacted knot, to the man standing untouched at its rim, and he ran the comparison he ran on every living thing he met, the arithmetic that had kept him alive since the Underground: *can I reach him. Can I cut him. What happens if I'm wrong.*

For the first time in his life, all three columns came back the same answer, which was: *it does not matter what you do.*

His hand, only Mikasa was close enough to see it, was not perfectly still.

And Erwin Smith — who had spent the day spending human lives at the only exchange rate that bought his country tomorrows, who had watched the deaths and filed every one of them as both a wound and a data point, because that was the tax on his particular obsession — stood at the center of the command group, utterly still, his tall frame motionless as a portrait, and looked at the man on the plain, and thought, with the cold total clarity that was his gift and his curse:

*The whole board just changed.*

Not the enemy. Not the walls. Not the government he served and suspected, the secrets in the basement of the world he was excavating one expedition at a time, the plan into which he had fed one hundred and sixty lives *today alone*. All of it — every calculation, every ally, every enemy, every one of humanity's four years of remaining arithmetic — had just been renegotiated by a single factor no ledger contained.

*I'm not looking at a soldier,* Erwin thought. *I'm looking at what every government in history has dreamed of owning and had nightmares of facing. If the capital learns of this — when the capital learns of this—* The thought opened under him like a stairwell. He didn't flinch from it. He never flinched from stairwells. He simply began, then and there, standing in the steam and the silence, to plan around it.

On the plain, the horde broke.

Not all at once — nothing that size dies at once — but the pressure went out of it. The convergence lost its faith. Titans at the edges of the seething mass began to do what Titans had done for a century: mill, wander, split off toward the nearest human smells, the ancient stupid hunger reasserting itself over whatever had summoned them. The sea broke into slicks. The ridge's exhausted squads began to cut them down at the margins, blade by blade, the old work, and the man in black walked through the middle of the dissolving army like a tourist leaving a festival before the fireworks, and every monster within thirty meters of his path simply fell as he passed, napes opening, bodies steaming, until even the dullest survivor on that hill understood the plain was his and the day was over.

It was 12:52 in the afternoon. The Survey Corps had lost one hundred and sixty of its members.

It had, by any accounting its survivors would ever manage, gained the rest of their lives, and something else besides — something that had fallen out of the sky, was now standing in the grass fifty meters below the command crest, yawning.

Actually yawning: arms stretching over his head, spine arching, a cracking sound audible up the slope, jaw cracking around a yawn of magnificent rudeness while the steam of a thousand dissolving corpses drifted past him like morning fog.

Gojo had, in fact, seen enough. He'd confirmed the monsters, confirmed the humans, confirmed the walls and the fear and the little person-candles and the something-hungry-underneath-it-all — a whole world of it, a *farm* of it, was the shape forming at the back of his mind, a whole species penned in a pen somebody built, and whoever built the pen must love something about all this fear, and Gojo Satoru would, at some point, like a word with them —

— and underneath the cataloguing, in the room where he was always alone, there was something else, something he'd have denied with his whole chest and his best grin: the static that had lived in him since a girl died on a stone floor and his best friend walked away smiling — the flat gray hum of a strongest who had run out of surprises — had gone *quiet*. Not gone. Quiet. Like snow settling. The world, some world, still had things in it worth seeing.

He put the thought down carefully, like something with a cracked edge, because the hill was coming to him.

They came down the crest in force — the survivors of the formation, called into a loose crescent by whistled signals, blades out and gas canisters rattling empty, every soldier who could still stand: and at their point, dropping the last ten meters of slope on a single controlled burst of steam and wire, landed a short man in a cravat with a face like a knife kept promises.

Levi landed five meters from Gojo, blades low, weight settled, and did not introduce himself, because introductions were for people who might spare the time.

"Tch." Levi's eyes went over him once, head to foot, missing nothing, the blindfold, the unhurt hands, the clothes without a single stain of blood or earth on them in a field soaked in both. "So the sky spat something out. Naturally it starts slaughtering Titans. Nothing in this world is ever simple."

"Nothing?" Gojo tilted his head, delighted. "You sound like a guy with stories. I *love* stories." He raised a finger before Levi's blades could decide anything. "Also — you're strong, right? Like, famously? I can tell. You stand like someone who's used to being the fastest thing in the room." He grinned, blindfold and all, the expression arriving like sunlight through a crack. "I know that stance. I *invented* that stance."

Levi stared at him.

"...Who the hell are you," Levi said, which was not one of the eleven questions he'd planned, but the day had earned it.

"Gojo Satoru." He said it the way you'd give a hotel your name at check-in. "Sorcerer. Special grade — that's, ah, their words, honestly the grading system's kind of arbitrary—" he waved the hand, dismissing entire bureaucracies, "—currently from very far away. Upper atmosphere, most recently. I'm new. Be gentle." 

"You fell out of a hole in the sky," Levi said flatly.

"I did! Good eyes. Ten out of ten for the observation." Gojo pointed upward with his chin. "Big tear, lots of light, I have questions about it too, we can compare notes. I also have questions about the giants, the walls, the pen— the *walls*—" he caught himself, cheerfully, revealing nothing, "—and where a man gets lunch, in that order, but honestly I'd flex on the order."

Behind Levi, the crescent of survivors had come down the slope in utter silence, and now stood arrayed in the steaming field in a ragged horseshoe — two hundred exhausted, grieving, bloodstained soldiers, staring at a man who talked like a festival and had deleted a thousand monsters before their eyes.

And because the world owed them one more impossibility, it was then — with the survivors gathered, with Levi's blades still out, with Erwin Smith striding down the crest toward them with his eyes already calculating — that Gojo Gojo-hooked a thumb under the black cloth, and pulled the blindfold off.

Armin Arlert was the closest recruit to him, close enough to see clearly, and Armin forgot, for the second time in his life, how to breathe.

The eyes were blue. That was the wrong word, and Armin knew every word he had. The sky over the walls was blue. These were what the sky was a *picture of* — a depth of blue that didn't reflect the daylight so much as consider it, two points of atmosphere-colored light in a young, friendly, human face, and they swept the horseshoe of soldiers once, unhurried, and every soldier they crossed felt it, unmistakably, as *being read* — not threatened, not hunted. Read. The way a page is read.

The cloth hung loose around his neck. The wind moved his white hair. Steam drifted through the space behind him, and the noon light came down through it all, and the man in the middle of the ruin of the horde looked at the last soldiers of humanity with an expression none of them could file — not a titan's hunger, not a commander's hunger, something younger and older at once, curiosity the size of a wound.

"...Huh," Gojo said again, softly, to the wall of fear facing him — two hundred survivors who had given everything they had and been carried the last distance by something they had no name for, standing in the smoke of their dead and their saved, staring back.

And something in his chest — call it a teacher's disease, call it the thing that had made him collect a ten-year-old with a dead-fish glare — snagged, hard, on a face near the front of the crowd: a green-eyed boy his own little brother's age, blood-crusted, handcuffed to his guard, tears cutting clean lines down through the grime on his face, wearing an expression Gojo had seen exactly once before, in a mirror, the day he'd learned what the word *strongest* would cost.

*Oh no,* Gojo thought, with a sinking, fatal feeling. *He's interesting.*

"Some survey," he said out loud, to all of them, spreading his empty hands, and smiled — a real one, crooked, absurd, human, radiant, terrifying — while the steam rose and the wings of freedom hung tattered over the ridge and sixty kilometers of Wall stood quiet on the horizon, holding its breath and its secrets and its gods. "Anyone want to tell me where a guy can get something to eat around here? I'm *starving.*"

Silence. Wind. The hiss of a thousand Titans becoming weather.

Somewhere behind him Sasha Blouse whispered, hoarsely, reverently, to Connie Springer: "I don't care what he eats. I'm adopting him."

And far beneath the ground they all stood on — beneath the walls and the graves and the plains and the packed-earth bowl already cooling in the light — in a place that had no distance and no direction, where all the terror of a whole species ran like sand through the fingers of something that had been waiting a very long time, that waiting thing turned the smallest fraction of its vast attention toward a tear that had opened and closed in its sky, and toward the man who had come through it.

For the first time in a hundred years, the paths under the world were not silent.

History, which had been marching in a straight line toward a basement in Shiganshina, had just walked into a wall that wasn't on any map.

It did not get up for a while.
